# ANYTHING SHARP FACING DOWN

[AUTHOR NAME]

### Proem

There are two of everything.

The machine makes a file each day, and on the Friday the week goes to Josie, and what she does with it after that is the book. The book has a title I did not choose, and it will be reviewed and quoted and read aloud at festivals, and it is what I said.

This is the other one.

In the mornings, between five and eight, before the house is up, I sit with the pages of the day before, which Josie prints for me before she goes because I asked her to. For checking, I said. She believed me. I put back what came out. I set down what I said instead of what I meant, and I mark the difference, and the hand is worse than it was in March, and I have stopped minding, because nobody is going to read this for the handwriting.

Nobody knows I am doing it. By the end of this book you will understand that this is the natural condition of anything I do.

One of them will be published. The other one is in the second drawer of the table in the morning room, underneath a card from a woman called Denise, and if you are reading this, then somebody has found the drawer, and I have decided not to mind who.

### One

We moved to the house on Cliff Rise in the spring of 1961.

Sixty-two. It was sixty-two, because my brother was already walking by then, and there is a photograph of him on the front step in a coat that was mine first, and he is standing up in it, holding the railing, looking at something out of frame. My mother is behind him with her hand not quite touching his shoulder. She had a way of doing that. Her hand would be an inch from you for a long time before it landed, if it landed.

Four. There were four of us if you count my grandmother, and everybody counted my grandmother.

The house was pale yellow. I want to say ochre, because ochre is the word my mother used and she used it in a particular voice, but it was the yellow that public buildings were in those years, the yellow of a post office or the waiting room where you went about somebody's pension, and calling it ochre was part of a larger project of hers which I did not understand until I was much older, and by then it was too late to be unkind about it.

She was very good at it. I have never met anybody better.

Bracebridge Heath, if you want it precisely, which I imagine you do. Up on the cliff, south of Lincoln, on the limestone, where the land stops pretending and goes flat for forty miles.

There were eleven houses on Cliff Rise. Ours was the fourth from the end, or the fourth from the beginning, depending which way you were walking, and we always said fourth from the end, because the end was where the Sissons lived and the Sissons were the reason anybody would be counting.

The street had a system and the system was never once described aloud in my hearing. The four houses at the Sisson end had garages. The seven at ours had what the developer's plan called a hardstanding, which is a rectangle of concrete with an ambition. Everybody on that street knew the difference and everybody on that street would have been amazed to be told it mattered. When the Ranholmes put in a garage in 1968, the street agreed, spontaneously and without discussion, that it was on account of his knee.

I have been asked to begin with a house, and I understand why. A house is a safe place to start. Nobody has ever been sued over a description of a kitchen.

The house before that one was down in Lincoln, off Newland, near enough to the water tower that we gave the water tower as the address, and I remember almost nothing about it that I have not been given. I have my mother's version, which is a very good version and which I have repeated in company for fifty years. I have a smell, which is bitumen, which means it is probably the road and not the house at all. And I have one true thing, which is the sound the front gate made, and which I have never heard since, not once, not in any other gate anywhere in the world, and I have listened.

That is what a childhood is, mostly. One noise you can't get back and a great deal of other people's furniture.

---

My father sold insurance, and then he sold something adjacent to insurance, and then for the last eleven years of his working life he did not sell anything at all but managed the people who did, which he called a promotion, and which paid, I later established, a hundred and forty pounds a year more. He had a new title with four words in it. Two of the words were the same word in different forms. He was very pleased, and he was right to be pleased, because the title outlived the firm, the firm having been absorbed in 1979 by a larger one that was itself absorbed in 1991, and I have seen that title on a laminated card in a drawer in Newark as recently as March.

He left the house at ten past seven. He came back between six and half past. Between those two facts he was, to me, entirely theoretical.

I do not say that with bitterness and I would ask you to take that out if it reads as though I do. He was a kind man who was somewhere else. That whole street was full of kind men who were somewhere else. They departed within ten minutes of one another in the morning, in coats, carrying almost identical cases, and they returned in a rather less coordinated way, and in between, the women stayed and made the arrangements.

And then in the evening the women told them the day.

Not the day. A version of the day, which took four minutes, sometimes six, delivered from the kitchen or the kitchen doorway while the man sat down and put his feet somewhere and made the noise men made in that decade when they sat down. The version was accurate. I want to be fair to my mother, whose versions were extremely accurate and who never in her life said a false thing that could be checked. It simply had things taken out of it, and the things taken out were the ones that could not be got into a sentence in four minutes, which is a category with no name and, it turns out, no bottom.

Then he formed his opinions on it. He would say what he thought about the school, or my grandmother's doctor, or the Ranholmes. He said it seriously. He had a good mind, my father, and he applied it faithfully, every evening, at a quarter to seven, to a four-minute summary prepared by an interested party, and he considered the result to be his own view of his own household, and so did she, and so, for a very long time, did I.

Everybody found this satisfactory. That is the part I would ask you to sit with, if you have time. Nobody was deceived. Nobody complained. It worked, in the sense that the houses stayed up and the children were fed and Ronnie Skerritt came with his ladder and did the gates every third year, and if you had gone along Cliff Rise in 1962 asking the eleven men whether they knew what went on in their own homes, all eleven would have said yes, and been offended, and been sincere.

I was nine years old and I could have given you a list.

---

The aerodrome was two miles south and I have to be careful here, because everybody from that part of the county says the same thing about it and it has become a sort of local boast.

What is true is this. The bombers came over low enough that you learned to wait. Not to stop, particularly, and not to look up, because looking up marked you out as somebody's cousin from Nottingham. You waited. A conversation would open a gap in itself and close again afterwards and nobody would acknowledge that the gap had happened. My grandmother, who came to us from a flat above a chemist's in Sleaford and who had opinions about everything, never once mentioned them, and she had been living under them for six years by the time she died.

I stopped hearing them at some point. I could not tell you when. That is the thing about it that I find I want to write down, and I am aware it is not much of an observation. You cannot notice the moment you stop noticing something. There is no moment. There is a year in which you can still hear it and a later year in which you cannot, and nothing in between that anybody could point to.

They are still flying out of there. Different things now, obviously.

---

My grandmother had the back room, which had been the dining room and which went on being called the dining room throughout the entire period in which nobody dined in it. She had come to us. That was the phrase. Coming to us was something that happened to her rather than anything done by anybody, in the way that weather comes to a county.

She was not unhappy, and I want to be accurate about this, because it has become fashionable in the last twenty years to go back and find everybody miserable. She was disappointed, which is different, and which you can live inside quite comfortably for a very long time provided you have a chair and a window.

She had both. The window gave on to the Sissons' hedge and my grandmother conducted a commentary on that hedge for six years. I do not mean she mentioned it. I mean she followed it the way other people followed a serial, with a memory for its previous behaviour and a firm sense of what it owed her. The hedge was getting away with things. The hedge was doing better than it deserved. There was a fortnight in the spring of 1963 when the hedge was being neglected, and my grandmother's tone about this was the tone of a woman watching a marriage fail in public, and I sat with her some afternoons and agreed with her, and I was ten, and it was the most sophisticated conversation available to me in that house.

She died in 1968, in the chair. My mother said she had gone very peacefully, and she had, and my mother also said it in a way that closed the subject, and it stayed closed. We began eating in the dining room again in about 1970, and my mother would say, when anyone came, that of course they had always eaten in there, and nobody ever contradicted her, and I am no longer certain she was even lying. Two years is enough. Two years will do it.

---

I was good at school in the way that causes the least possible trouble.

I would like to put that better. I was good at school and I made sure nobody found it interesting. There is a particular mark, the second-best one, which purchases total invisibility, and I found it early, and I should like to set out what it buys, because I have never seen it written down.

It buys you this. Teachers who are pleased with you and never think about you. Parents who mention you at Christmas in one clause. Other girls who do not have to decide anything about you. It costs nothing to maintain, it never has to be defended, and it removes you permanently from the list of people who might be asked to do something.

You have to work at it, though. That is the part nobody says. It is not idleness. I had to know precisely how good the best answer would have been in order to come in reliably underneath it, which meant producing the best answer privately first, at the desk, at home, and then sanding it. I got very quick at the sanding. By the third year I could do it while writing, in real time, which is a skill I have since used at perhaps four hundred dinners.

Once, in the first year at the high school, I answered a question in maths correctly and at length and with a small flourish at the end that I had prepared the night before without admitting to myself that I was preparing it. It took perhaps forty seconds. A boy called Alan Rands turned round in his chair and looked at me for about two seconds with an expression I can still see, and it was not cruelty and it was not even dislike. It was the look of a man reading a sign in a language he had not known was spoken locally.

I understood the arrangement completely and instantly and I never did it again. Not once, in six years.

I don't blame Alan Rands. He was twelve. He is dead now, and he sold agricultural machinery out of a yard on the Sleaford road for thirty years and was by every account very good at it, and at his funeral in 2004 his wife Sylvia took my hand and told me he had always spoken of me warmly and had been so proud, afterwards, of having known me. I said the correct thing. A woman called Brenda Kitchin, who had done the flowers and whom I have never met before or since, told me on the way out that I looked exactly like my mother, which was not true and was meant beautifully. I have been saying the correct thing at funerals since I was eleven.

What I mean is that I was not silenced. Nobody silenced me. I have listened to a great many women of my generation describe being silenced and I believe every one of them, and it did not happen to me, and I have occasionally wished it had, because then there would be somebody to point at.

I did it myself. I did it for sixty years, and some of that praise is on television.

---

Mr Cansdale taught mathematics to the fourth and fifth years and he thought a great deal of me.

I want that first, because everything I am about to say will look like an accusation if I put it the other way round, and it is not one. He was a good teacher in a school that did not have many, he stayed on after four o'clock without being asked, and he is the reason I know what a proof is. He had a hare lip which had been repaired at some point before the war and which he was self-conscious about in a way that made him talk slightly too fast, and I was fifteen before I stopped hearing it and about forty before I understood what it had probably cost him.

In the February of 1969 he kept me back and told me what I ought to do.

He said I had a very tidy mind. He said it twice, and he meant it as the highest thing he had, and I have thought about that phrase for fifty-seven years. He said that the girls who went off to read mathematics tended to come back, and that there was a great deal of loose talk about it at present, and that what I wanted was a subject where the work was set out in front of me and where being reliable would be noticed. He suggested pharmacology. He said there would always be laboratories.

I said that sounded very sensible. I remember being warm about it. I remember producing two follow-up questions about entry requirements, designed to demonstrate that I had already been thinking along those lines, which I had not.

What I had been thinking along was mathematics, and I had been thinking along it since I was eleven, and I had a notebook.

I did not say so. He would have listened. That is the part I would ask you to hold on to: he would have listened, he would very probably have changed his advice, and if he had not changed it he would at least have argued with me, and being argued with by Mr Cansdale would have been the most interesting forty minutes of my adolescence.

I went home and told my mother he had recommended pharmacology and she said that sounded very sensible, and my father said at a quarter to seven that it sounded very sensible, and that was that, and I have never in my life been so completely agreed with.

He sent a card when Martin became leader. It came to the constituency office and was forwarded on, and it said he had followed it all with the greatest interest and had always known I would go far. He was eighty-one. The handwriting was very good.

I wrote back warmly, and I meant it.

*The notebook went in the bin in the July of 1971, with a great deal else, and I did it myself, and it took no time at all.*

---

Here is what I actually want to say about the house on Cliff Rise, and I have been going round it for six pages, which I notice.

We left it in the summer I turned eighteen. The reason we left, as everybody in my family knows and as I have said myself at three funerals and one wedding, is that my father's back had got worse and the stairs were bad and the plot at Waddington Fields was cheaper and my mother had always wanted a garden with the afternoon sun.

Every part of that is true. The stairs were bad, and steep, and turned twice. His back was bad and got worse and was operated on in 1981 with a result everyone described as good. The new plot was cheaper by eleven per cent, a figure I have from the papers and not from anybody's mouth. My mother wanted the afternoon sun and got it and grew almost nothing in it for thirty years except, eventually, a hedge, which she talked about.

We left because of the Sissons.

I knew it at nine, which is when it began. I knew it completely at twelve. I have never said it aloud in a room where it could have been contradicted. My mother went to her grave with the back and the stairs and the afternoon sun. My brother, who was ten, has the back and the stairs and the afternoon sun, and he is sixty-five, and he will die with them, and there is nothing wrong with that. It is a perfectly serviceable account. It has held up under fifty-five years of continuous use, which is a great deal more than can be said for the house, and I have contributed to its maintenance at every opportunity, including twice this year.

I am not going to explain the Sissons. I have thought about it and the answer is no. It would take a chapter and it would be the wrong chapter, and there are two people still living who could work out who they were, and one of them has been perfectly decent to me.

I mention it only so that you know I am the kind of person who can hold a thing like that for sixty years without any effort at all. It costs me nothing. I don't lie awake over it. I have never wanted to tell anybody, not once, not even during the weeks when telling somebody something would have been the ordinary human thing to do.

You should know that about me early. It seems only fair.

---

My mother's project, the one with the ochre, was to arrange our family very slightly to the left of where it actually stood.

She never claimed anything untrue, and this is the thing I have inherited most completely, so I want to set the mechanism out properly, because it is a real technique and it can be taught.

You do not lie. Lying is for people with no vocabulary. You select. My father was *with* a firm, never *at* one. My grandmother had *come to us*. We had *taken* the house on Cliff Rise, which is what you say when you have bought the cheapest one on the street. The Ranholmes' garage was *on account of his knee*, and the extraordinary thing is that she believed that, or believed it in the way that mattered, which is that she never checked.

And then, when required, you deploy. When the Sissons' girl was admitted to read law, my mother said, in the kitchen, to a neighbour, with real warmth: 'Well, of course, they've wanted it so badly for her.'

There is not a false word in that sentence. It contains no claim of any kind. It is warm, it is generous, it takes eight words, and by the time it has crossed the room it has established that the girl got in because her parents pushed, that the pushing was faintly unseemly, and that we, in this kitchen, would never. I was eleven. I remember the feeling exactly and it was not disapproval. It was the feeling you get watching somebody park a car in a space you would not have attempted.

*Oh*, I thought. *You can do that.*

I could do it by twelve. By fifteen I could do it better than she could, and I never once did it in front of her, because doing it in front of her would have been doing it to her, and that was not the arrangement. I did it at school, sparingly. I did it at Nottingham. I did it for twenty-five years in the phrasing of recommendations that had to go up and had to fail on the way, and I was known for being fair.

Nobody taught me anything else. I want to be careful here, because a woman of my age describing her mother is walking towards a warm bath of a certain kind and I can feel the water round my ankles. She was not a monster, she was not a victim, and she did not stunt me. She gave me the only tool in the house. It was a superb tool and I have used it every day of my life since, in kitchens, at receptions, at two state funerals, and in one particular conversation lasting between six and nine minutes on a Tuesday morning in the spring of the year that everybody reading this has bought the book for.

We will get to it. It is in here. I have not left it out.

---

The kitchen at Cliff Rise had a window over the sink that did not open, a back door that opened but stuck in wet weather, a table with one leaf, and a floor of red-brown tiles, eight rows by fourteen, which I counted so often that I can still produce the number without doing the sum.

My mother stood at that sink approximately four hours a day. I have done the arithmetic and I am confident about the four.

She washed everything twice. She washed it once herself, and then, on the occasions when my father had washed something, which was Sundays and Christmas and the two days after each of her operations, she did it again. She did not do it straight away, and that is the detail I would ask you to keep. She waited. She let the things stand on the rack for half an hour, sometimes an hour, and then took them down and washed them and put them back in the same order, with her back to the room and without comment, and my father, in his chair, twelve feet away, reading, never saw it happen. Not once. In nine years.

The only part of it she ever put into words was the knives. Anything sharp facing down. She said it to me, at the sink, once, in passing, as though she were handing something over. He stood them up on his Sundays, points in the air, and she turned them over, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it ran for nine years, and neither of them would have called it one.

He would sometimes say, on those Sundays, that he had done the washing-up. She would say that he had, and she would be telling the truth, and I would be at the table with my books in front of me, doing my sanding.

I saw it every time.

I want to say that I found it sad. That is the sentence. I have used it about her in public more than once and it does very well. People nod. Somebody says something about their own mother. It has never failed.

I didn't find it sad. I found it interesting, and I filed it, and I was nine or ten years old, and I understood without being able to put it into words that I had just been shown where a man cannot see.

Not what he cannot see. Where.

---

I never asked her whether she knew that I knew. There were about four thousand evenings in which I could have, and one afternoon in 1994 in a side room at Lincoln County when it would have taken nothing at all, and I have no explanation, and I am not going to construct one now.

---

That house is gone. Not knocked down. Rendered, extended at the back, painted a grey I am told is very popular, and the hardstanding taken up and replaced with gravel, which on that street, in that position, is a joke that nobody living there now could possibly hear.

I went past it in 2019, in the car, with somebody else driving, and I did not ask him to slow down. I looked at it for as long as the road allowed, which was about four seconds, and then we were past it and he was talking about something at the department, and I said the thing you say.

The gate has gone too. They have a different gate.

I listened.

### Two

There is a machine on the table between us about the size of a bar of soap, and a red light on it, and I have been assured that I will stop noticing the red light.

I have not stopped noticing the red light.

We work in the morning room, which faces east and is therefore finished as a room by about eleven, and we start at half past nine because that is when I am at my best, and we stop at half past eleven whether I want to or not. Two hours. It was put to me as a recommendation and I have observed that it is not one.

I can tell you what the boundary is like, since I am the only person who will ever stand on it. At about the hundredth minute the sentences begin to arrive pre-assembled, from stock, and they are good stock, mine, but I can no longer feel the join where the sentence meets the thing it is about. It is like driving in fog on a road you know: you can do it, beautifully, for miles, and you should not, and the passengers cannot tell the difference, which is the frightening part, and Josie stops us at the point where I can still tell, which means she is watching for something in my face that I would give a great deal to know the name of.

Josie sits in the wing chair with her feet tucked up, which I like very much and would not have dared at her age in somebody else's house. She is thirty-one. She has done nine of these. Two footballers, a chef, a woman off the television whose name I did not know and pretended to, a general, and three I am not to mention. She is extremely good and I did not expect that. I had expected somebody to take dictation and instead I have somebody who says, on about the fourth morning, that the grandmother is doing a great deal of work and the brother is doing none, and who is right.

No. Leave it in. If it comes out later it comes out later, but leave it in for now.

What she is not is interested in politics. Not hostile to it, not bored by it, simply not oriented that way, the way some people are not oriented towards music. She asked me in the first week, entirely without edge, how many years Martin had actually been in office, and I told her, and she wrote it on the back of something.

I found that restful. I have wondered why, and the answer is not flattering to me, and I will come to it.

She has asked me three times now about my mother. Specifically about 1994, and specifically about the side room, and each time she has asked it as though it had just occurred to her.

I have said very little and she has written it all down.

---

The publisher wants four things and has been admirably direct about all of them.

They want the early material, because there is a market at present for women of my age describing a country that no longer exists, and the yellow house and the second-best mark and the hedge are, I am told, exactly the thing.

They want the marriage, but not the difficulties, or rather the difficulties in the tone one uses at a memorial service.

They want the years in office from the inside, by which they mean the rooms, the dinners, what people were actually like. Not the policy. Nobody has ever wanted the policy.

I will put the policy down anyway, once, later in this book, when we are in the building, because he has earned a page and nobody else is going to give him one.

And they want the strike.

They want the morning, two springs ago, on which my husband personally authorised the killing of a British citizen on Russian soil. Personally, because the man held a British passport, and for that the law wants a name and not a committee. And what they want from me, specifically, although nobody has put it in these words, is what he was like at breakfast.

They want it and they are right to want it, and I have known since the first meeting that everything else in this book is scaffolding around one chapter that people will turn to first in the shop. My editor did not put it like that. My editor said that readers would come to the book with an existing relationship to that period, which is a very good sentence and I have thought about stealing it.

The title is theirs. *A Quiet Kind of Life*. I wanted something drier and I suggested three things and they were all, on reflection, the sort of title that sells four thousand copies to people who already agree with you. I let it go.

I let it go the way I let things go, which is completely, and immediately, and with a small private note made of the fact that I had let it go, filed somewhere I do not read.

Except once. There was a fourth, which I offered at the second meeting and watched die on the table with full honours: Whitlow, my editor, said it was 'marvellous' and 'so you' and wrote it down with a fountain pen, at an angle, on the corner of a pad, which is where titles go to die, and by the next meeting it had never existed. I did not fight for it, because I had already understood what I wanted it for, and it was not their spine. It is on the front of this one, in my hand, on the first page in the drawer, and you have already read it, because you read it before you read anything else, and it is the only title I have ever held out for in my life, and I held out for it in secret.

There is a photograph on the front of theirs, of me at about fifty, in blue, at something. It was taken by a man called Anders Holm whom I met for eleven minutes and who told me to think about a room I liked, and I thought about the room over the boiler at Nottingham, and that is the expression.

Whitlow took me to lunch once, early, to a place with a cloakroom for coats that had never been rained on, and spent the first course being intelligent about other people's memoirs and the second being delicate about mine, and over coffee he said, 'What we're all hoping, Alison — may I — is that you'll feel able to be *free*,' and gave the word a little room of its own, and I said I intended to be entirely honest, which is not the same undertaking, and he was too pleased to notice, and the contract was signed by the Friday, and that is the only lie I have told him, and I told it before the soup.

---

I am ill. Let us do this now and then largely leave it alone, because a book in which somebody is dying by instalments is a book with one gear.

It is in the liver and it came from somewhere else and the somewhere else was found late, and the position is that there is a length of time, and the length of time has a range, and the range has been given to me twice in slightly different terms by two people who were both being kind.

Dr Beck was the second of the two. She is not much older than Hannah and wears the manner they are taught now, which is candour with the corners taken off, and when I asked her to say it again in other terms, because I collect terms, she looked at me for a moment to check what I was actually asking for, and saw, and said: 'Plant the garden for somebody else to see it.' I said thank you, and meant it, and we have got on extremely well ever since on the basis of that one sentence, which did the thing a range cannot do, which is fit in a drawer. I have decided to be seventy-four. That is not a prediction. It is a piece of scheduling.

Josie knows. Martin knows. The publisher knows, which is why the schedule is what it is, and which is why nobody has argued with me about anything as much as I expected.

That is the true reason for the two hours, incidentally, and it should be said plainly once: not because I tire, though I do, but because after two hours I stop being able to hear the difference between a sentence I mean and a sentence that merely sounds like me. That is the faculty going first. Of all the things that might have gone first.

What it is actually like, since nobody has asked and since I have read a great many accounts that decline to say.

The mornings have a chemistry to them now, sorted by Karen on her days into a strip of small boxes with the days printed on them — Tuesday's visit covers Wednesday to Friday, Friday's covers the weekend and the Monday and the Tuesday, and the arithmetic has never once failed — and I take each day's in reverse order of size, which is not an instruction, as a private joke with nobody, and then the tea, and then this.

I wake at about five and I am at my clearest between then and eight, which is a joke of a kind, because those are the hours in which there is nothing to be clear about. There is an ache under the right ribs which is not pain and which I have never found a word for. It is more like being aware of an organ, the way you are aware of a tooth. It has been there fourteen months and I have stopped noticing it in the way I stopped noticing the aircraft, and then it moves and I notice it again.

I itch. That is the one nobody mentions. Not a rash, nothing to see. The skin of my shins and my forearms, in the evenings, from the inside, and there is a tablet for it that works about half the time.

I am the colour of a bad photograph. I have lost two stone and it has gone from my face and my upper arms and from nowhere else, so that I look like a woman wearing a larger woman's body, which I find funny and which I have not said to anybody because it is the sort of remark that makes people take your hand.

I sleep in the afternoons and dream constantly and the dreams are extremely dull. Rooms, and lists, and a persistent one about a filing cabinet.

And I am hungry. Ravenously, stupidly hungry, three or four times a day, for salt and for vinegar and for things I have not eaten since 1974, and I can manage about a third of anything put in front of me. That has been the cruellest of it, and I am aware of how that sounds set against everything else, and it is still true.

---

*I am not frightened. I have waited for it in the way you wait for the noise of an aircraft you have learned the sound of, and it has not come, and it is possible that it will not come, and it is possible that this is simply what I am like.*

---

I have been asked not to drive.

Not forbidden. Nobody forbids you anything at this stage, they simply describe the position and leave you with it, which is a technique I recognise and admire.

We came to Rutland when it ended because it is nobody's county, which was the point. It is an hour from the house I grew up in. I have decided that is a coincidence.

I went out in April. I told Martin I was going to Pat's and I did not go to Pat's. I went east instead, and crossed into Lincolnshire past South Witham, and got up onto the cliff road where you can see eleven miles and there is nothing in any of it, and I drove for about two hours and did not stop, and came back before the light went.

I have done that since I was twenty. Never with anybody. Never anywhere in particular. I have gone past the turning I meant to take perhaps three hundred times in fifty years and I have never once been late for anything as a result, which tells you something about the size of my life and possibly about the size of me.

And on the way home you buy chips and you eat them in the car in a lay-by with the engine off, and you put the paper in the bin at the top of the road and you go in and you say it was lovely at Pat's.

Four or five times a year. For fifty years. I have never told anybody that and I have no idea why not, because there is nothing in it. It is the only thing I have ever kept from people that did not need keeping.

---

Now. What this book is for.

I want to say it properly because I have not said it to anybody, including Josie, and I want it down before the ordinary business of the thing wears the intention down, which I am told it does.

I have never told the truth about myself to anyone.

Not lies. I want to be exact, because I have already set out in the previous chapter how my mother managed this and I am not claiming to have improved on her. I have never made a false statement of any consequence in my life. I have simply never said the thing, and there has never been a single occasion on which anybody required me to, and the second half of that sentence is the part that has kept me awake in recent months.

Sixty years. Parents, brother, friends, colleagues, two selection panels, a husband, four doctors, and a woman in 1994 whom I will come to, and not one of them ever got to the bottom of it, and I did not make it difficult for them. It was there the whole time. It is not even hidden particularly well. Anybody could have had it for the asking, and nobody asked, and I have spent a great deal of my life feeling superior about that and I no longer do.

So: this. Four hundred pages, a young woman who is good at her job, and a machine the size of a bar of soap. This is the last available opportunity and I intend to use it, and I have said so out loud now, in a room, with the light on.

I mean it entirely. I want that noted, because I know how this reads. Every memoir opens with somebody promising to be honest and every reader has learned to discount it, and I would discount it too. I can only tell you that I have never wanted anything the way I want this, that I have wanted almost nothing in my life with any force at all, and that I am aware those two facts sit oddly together.

---

Martin comes in at about ten.

He does not mean to. He comes in looking for something, and having come in he stays, and he stands with the thing he was looking for in his hand for a period of between four and twenty minutes. He has been retired since the spring of last year and the days are long, and he has found that this room has people in it.

Last Thursday it was nineteen minutes and I am going to set it down, because Josie has the recording and because I have noticed that I describe him a great deal and never simply let him be in a room.

He came in at four minutes past ten with the post, which he had already opened, and a pair of secateurs. He put the secateurs on the windowsill, where they still are. He said, 'Don't stop, don't stop,' which is what he always says, and we stopped.

He asked Josie whether her sister had got the flat. She had not. He said the right thing about that, which was short and not sympathetic and made her laugh, and then he asked which one it had been, the one over the shop or the other one, and it was the one over the shop, and he said he'd thought it would be, and that he'd not liked the sound of the man.

Then he stood by the window with the post and told us about the surveyor.

There is a surveyor coming about the roof, and there has been a surveyor coming about the roof since March, and the account of the surveyor's non-arrival has now been given to me a dozen times and is getting better. He does it well. It has acquired a supporting character in the form of a woman at the firm called Trisha. Josie was properly entertained and I could see him notice that and put a bit more into the second half.

At about ten past he said, 'Where are we, then?' and I said 1962, and he said, 'Ah, the yellow house,' and then, after a moment, 'Sixty-two. Not sixty-one.'

I said I had already put sixty-two.

He said, 'Good. Because it was sixty-two,' and he began on why, which involves my brother in a coat, and I have heard it, and Josie had not, and he told it to her and it took three minutes and it was, I have to say, better than my version. There is a bit at the end about my mother and the photographer that I have never had.

Then he said he would leave us in peace, and he went as far as the door, and turned round and said, 'Are you all right for the tea,' and I said yes, and he said, 'Right,' and went.

He came back at half past ten with the tea and put it down slightly too far away from me.

He had forgotten the secateurs. They are still on the windowsill. I have not moved them and I do not intend to and I could not tell you why.

That is the first portrait of him in this book. It is accurate, and it is the version I would want found, and I have sat here for a while with the discovery that I cannot make those two sentences into one.

*He is right about people, quickly, from very little, in a way I have never been able to do. He was right about the man over the shop. He said to me afterwards in the kitchen that Josie is frightened of being found out as ordinary and that it makes her push too hard at the wrong bits, and that somebody ought to tell her she is already good enough, and that it will not be either of us.*

*Forty-four years I have watched him do that. He has never once done it to me.*

Other people come.

Karen comes on Tuesdays and Fridays and is Lithuanian and has been in England for nineteen years and is the only person in my life who has ever told me, flatly and without arranging it first, that I look worse than I did last week.

Dev brings the shopping on Wednesdays and will not accept anything and once, in the spring, stood in the porch for twenty minutes describing his father's citrus trees in Kampala. I have thought about that conversation more than about several I have had with Cabinet ministers, and I am aware that saying so is precisely the kind of thing people in my position say.

Colin comes over from Newark on the first Sunday of the month with two conservatory brochures, as a joke that is nine years old and still working, and sits with Martin for an hour talking about drainage, and with me for ten minutes not talking about anything, which is our way and always has been, and he holds my hand now, going, which is new, and neither of us remarks on it.

Pat Ellery comes when she can, which is more often now, and talks, mostly about Gillian, with whom she has been at war for ten years over a matter Gillian is right about, which Pat knows, and which I have never once said.

I met Pat in 1981 at a meeting about a proposed road, and we have had lunch four times a year for forty-five years, and she is the closest friend I have. She talks. She is very funny and entirely without malice and she has a memory for other people's grandchildren that I find frankly supernatural. In forty-five years and something over a hundred and eighty lunches she has never once asked me a question that required an answer, and I do not believe this has ever occurred to either of us until this month.

That is not a complaint about Pat. I want that understood. It is the arrangement I built and she came into it in good faith and has been loyal to it for half a century.

And Hannah, when the traffic allows, which it does not.

Hannah is my brother's girl, thirty-four, a physiotherapist in Nottingham, and she has admired me since she was about nine, and she has me completely wrong. She thinks I gave things up. She has said so, warmly, twice. She thinks there was a career I set down for Martin's sake and she is a little bit angry on my behalf about it, and I have never corrected her, because the correction is worse and because being admired for a sacrifice I did not make is the most comfortable position I have ever occupied.

She comes on Saturdays when the rota of her weekends allows, and does the pots, and reads the labels on the pills to make sure Karen and the surgery agree, which they always do, and she thinks I do not see her doing it, and I see everything, and I let her guard me, because being guarded by Hannah is the most restful experience of my old age: she has no theory of me at all. I am simply her aunt. There is nobody else left alive with so little imagination about me, and I could weep with the comfort of it.

She asked me something in March. I will come to it.

He has not asked to read it.

I have turned this over more than is reasonable. He was asked to write his own and refused, on the grounds that he had nothing to say that was not on the record already, which was true and was also, I think, the only entirely modest thing he has ever done. When mine was announced he was delighted. He rang people. And in seven months he has not once asked what is in it.

There are two explanations. One is that he assumes he knows what is in it, because he assumes he knows what is in me, and has done since about 1994.

The other is that he is being tactful, which he is capable of, and which I have never wholly been able to distinguish from the first thing.

I have decided not to find out. Note the verb.

---

We will do this chronologically. That was Josie's recommendation and it is the right one, although she framed it as a question in a way I have come to recognise as her method. Chronology, she says, is what people can hold. If you jump about, they spend their attention on the jumping.

I will jump about. Not much, and not on purpose. It is what happens when you talk. You reach 1971 and something in 1971 is joined to something in 2003 by a route nobody else can see, and you follow it, and you come back. She has said she will tidy it.

I am aware of what a memoir of this kind is expected to do with the year everybody has bought the book for. You save it. You approach it in a spiral, and the early chapters acquire a faint shadow, and by the time you arrive at it the reader has been made to want it.

I am not going to do that, and the reason is not a literary one.

It is that I have discovered, in the last few months, that I cannot make that year the point of my life. It is the point of everybody else's interest in my life. It is why there is a photograph of me at fifty on a jacket. But it was eight years out of seventy-three and I was in a great many rooms before it and a few after it, and if I build this book as an approach to a single morning then I will have agreed with them, and I find I am not willing to.

So it goes where it goes. In the order in which it happened. Somewhere after the middle.

I said no in the specific voice I have used since I was about eleven years old, and she wrote something down, and we moved on to the water tower.

### Three

I went up to Nottingham in the October of 1971, to a hall of residence smelling of polish and gravy, to a room with a desk, a bed, a basin and a radiator that worked, and on the first morning I ate breakfast alone at the end of a long table with a book propped against the milk jug, and nobody knew me, and nobody was coming, and I would like the scale of that happiness on the record, because it has never once been exceeded, only matched, and the things that matched it are all in this book, and they are all tables.

Dr Rennard, in the second week, filled three blackboards without notes, stood back, said, 'And the beautiful thing is—' and stopped, and looked at what he had written for a moment as though somebody else had done it. The room laughed. He did not, because he had not been joking, and I sat in the fourth row and knew, with the certainty you get twice in a life if you are lucky, that I was home, and I was right, and it lasted three years, and most people do not get the three years, so this paragraph is a receipt and not a complaint.

There will not be much about the leaving, because I have discovered in the last few weeks that I remember almost nothing about it, which I did not expect.

I have the fact of it. I have my father with the string. I do not have a single sentence anybody said, which is unusual for me, and I am not going to invent any.

---

Now something that will sit oddly here, and it should stay exactly where it falls.

In the second year at Nottingham I was very happy.

Not socially. Socially I did what I had always done, which is to be pleasant to about forty people and close to none of them.

There was one exception and her name was Christine Denby and she read geography, and she came into my room in the November of 1972 to complain about a man and stayed four hours, and at no point in those four hours did I say anything about myself, and she did not notice, and that became the shape of it for fifty-four years.

There was also a boy called Gordon.

I want to deal with this now because I have been putting it off for three sessions and because a book by a woman of my age which contains no account of any of it is a book with something missing from it, and I have read several and I could tell.

I liked him very much. He asked me once, in the May, in the dark, what I was thinking, honestly wanting to know, and I told him the truth, which was a recurrence relation, and he laughed until he had to sit up, and said, 'You're the only girl I know who'd say that,' delighted, entirely without grievance, and we were better friends from that night on, in the way you can be friends with a border once both sides have seen the map. We went about together for most of a year and there was a good deal of it and I was not shy about any of it, which surprised me at the time. I was extremely fond of my own body between about twenty and thirty-five, in a private and rather technical way, as a thing that worked.

What I did not have, then or since, was the other part. The wanting that people describe, where you are not in charge of it. I have listened to Christine on that subject for half a century and I have listened to Pat, and I have nodded, and I have contributed, and I have no idea what either of them is talking about. Not one idea. I assumed for fifty years that they were exaggerating and I have very lately begun to think that they were not.

That is not a sorrow. It has cost me nothing I can identify.

Gordon is not going to appear again.

What I mean by happy is the work.

There was a course in the second year, taught as an afterthought by Dr Rennard, lent to us from mathematics and clearly furious about having to do it, on the statistical analysis of biological data. Twelve of us. Thursday afternoons in a room over the boiler.

Ten men, one other woman, and me. The other woman was a Margaret Hebden, from Goole, who wore her coat through every session on account of the radiator, and who left at the end of that year to be married, and who handed me her notes at the door of the boiler room on her last Thursday, without ceremony, the way you hand on a parish. I have them still. They are better than mine. Nobody ever knew that either.

I found in about the third week that I could see it.

I do not know how else to put that and I have tried for two mornings. You are given a column of numbers which are the outcome of something that has actually happened to living creatures, and there is a shape in them, and the shape is not visible and is not a matter of opinion and is not going to argue with you, and if you do the work properly it comes up out of the noise like a name being called across a station. It is there or it is not there. Nobody's mother is impressed. There is no room in it of any kind for being liked.

I sat in that room over the boiler on Thursday afternoons in 1973 and I was as happy as I have ever been, and I include everything.

She has asked me to say more about that and I find I cannot, and we have been round it twice this morning and got no further, and I can hear myself starting to make it nice.

I was extremely good at it. Within about eighteen months I was better at it than the man teaching it, who knew, and who was decent about it, and who died in 1988, and whom I did not go and see even once after I left, although I meant to for twelve years.

Mathematics, incidentally. Which is what it was. I had got at it sideways through the back door of a subject a kind man had chosen for me, and I have never in fifty years told a single person that this was what had happened, including the man who chose it, and including Martin, and including the woman sitting six feet away from me with the recorder.

---

*Dr Rennard wrote to me in 1979 to ask whether I had thought of coming back and doing it properly. I answered by return and said that I was very settled. I have his letter. I do not have my reply and I know exactly what was in it.*

The notebook, for the record, since it has been in this book twice now and never once opened: squared paper, a red cover gone pink along the spine, and the last thing in it was a problem about partitions that I had found in a library book and could not let go of, worked over about thirty pages in a hand that got smaller as it got surer. At the bottom of the last page, age seventeen, I had written: *this must be known — check.* It was known. It had been known for two hundred years. I did not check, and that, more than the bin, is the epitaph: a girl who proved things privately and would not look up whether the world already had them, in case the world did, or in case it didn't. I have never worked out which possibility I was avoiding, and it is late to start, and both of them have the same shape from here.

---

I started driving that year.

Dennis Sallis, who was the technician on the second floor and who owned a Morris that he was contemptuous about, taught me in the car park behind the anatomy building over about six weekends and refused any money and accepted a bottle of something at Christmas for four years afterwards. I passed first time. He said I was the most frightening natural he had ever sat next to, because I did not chat.

He also said, at the end of the last lesson, wiping the windscreen with his sleeve, that most people drive the way they talk and I drove the way I listened, and that he meant it as a compliment and was not sure it was one. Dennis Sallis died in 1999. The Morris outlived him by a year, because his son could not bring himself to sell it, and then could.

The first time I went out on my own I meant to go into Nottingham and I came out of the car park and turned the other way, and I went out through Bingham and kept going, and I did not stop, and I got as far as Sleaford before I understood that I was heading for my parents' house and had no intention of arriving at it.

I turned round in a farm entrance about four miles short and I came back.

I did not think about anything on that drive. And I am precise about that, because it is the only claim in this book I am completely sure of. Not about Cansdale, not about the notebook, not about my mother. For two and a quarter hours there was the road and the wipers and nothing whatsoever, and I got back at ten past eleven and I have been doing it ever since.

---

Peter Aylmer supervised my final year and then kept me on for two years as a research assistant on money he found somewhere, and he was, at that time, forty-four, and rather good, and generous with his time in a way that was genuinely rare.

In the January of 1976 I put a paper on his desk. It was mine and it was fourteen pages and the argument in it was one I had had in the room over the boiler and worked up on my own for about nine months. He read it in front of me, which people did then, and he said it was very interesting, and he asked three questions, and the third question was excellent and I did not have an answer to it.

The paper that came out in the autumn of 1976 was his, with two others, and the argument was in it, and I was in the acknowledgements, in a list of four, in alphabetical order.

I have never believed that he stole it. I am being exact, because it would be a much better story if he had. He read forty things a month, he supervised nine people, he talked constantly, and I have watched from close quarters what happens to a man's memory when he is in the middle of a great deal of other people's thinking. By the autumn it was in him, and he had genuinely no idea where it had come from, and if I had walked into his room and said so he would have gone white and put my name on it and been ashamed for a decade.

I did not walk into his room.

But I went to it. That has never been in any version I have told, including the ones I told myself, and it is going in now.

The offprints came in a padded envelope in the October, to the lab, and I took mine to the corridor to read it, because the lab had people in it. It was a cold corridor with a radiator that did not work and a noticeboard with a fire drill notice on it and a card about a bicycle for sale, and I stood there and read fourteen pages I had already read, and then I walked the eleven steps to his door.

His light was on. I could hear him on the telephone, the shape of him on the telephone, the laugh he did for people who funded things. I stood outside that door with the offprint in my hand for the length of the call, which was some minutes, and I had the first sentence ready, and it was a good sentence, mild and unanswerable, and I had built it in the corridor without noticing I was building it, the way I once prepared a flourish for a mathematics lesson.

The call ended. There was the sound of a chair.

And I looked at the door, which was ordinary, brown, with his name in the little metal frame, and I understood, the way you understand a column of numbers, what was on the other side of it. Not a thief. A man who would go white. A man who would put my name on things for a decade out of shame, and hand me my idea back spoiled, and look at me, every day, for the rest of my time there, the way you look at somebody you owe.

I took the sentence apart standing in the corridor. I remember the dismantling as a physical relief, like setting down a case.

Then I knocked, and went in, and asked him about second-year demonstrating hours, which was a real question, and he answered it generously, because he was generous, and I said the offprint had come and it looked very handsome, and he said didn't it, and that was the whole of it, and I went back past the radiator and the bicycle card, and the corridor, I would estimate, took eleven years off something, and I have never worked out what the something was, and I have stopped estimating.

I have arranged the reasons for that many times. The best of them is that he had found the money for two years of my salary out of nothing at all, and I did not want to be the sort of person who took that and then made trouble. That reason is true. It is also the fourth or fifth thing I thought of.

The first thing I thought of, standing in the corridor with the offprint in my hand, was that if I said something he would look at me differently for the rest of the time I knew him, and that I would rather have the paper go than have him do that.

---

In 1981 I was at a dinner in Nottingham, eight people, somebody's leaving do, and a woman I did not know very well began on Aylmer. She said he had a habit. She said it flatly and without heat and she named two people it had happened to, neither of whom was me, and she was right.

I defended him.

I did it well. I did not say anything untrue. I said that I had worked for him for two years and had found him unusually generous, which was true, and I described the money he had found, which was true, and I said that a certain amount of that sort of talk followed anybody who was productive, which is a sentence that means nothing and which everybody at that table agreed with.

The woman let it go. She was not going to fight about it over a pudding with somebody who had actually worked for him.

Her name was Alma Gedge. She was, I think, in immunology. I have never seen her since. She surfaces about once a year, unbidden, which is more often than Aylmer does.

---

I went to Cattermole in the autumn of 1976 and I stayed four years.

Frank Loder ran the section. He was fifty-one, he wore a tie clip, he called everybody by their surname including the women, and he was, by the standards of that place at that time, entirely fair. I do not have a story about Frank Loder shouting at me. He never did. He gave me the difficult work because I was the one who could do it and he said so in front of people.

In 1979 the deputy's post came up and it went to Howard Slee, who was two years younger than me, who I had trained, and who was perfectly all right.

Frank called me in on the Tuesday afternoon. He did not have to do that and I want to set the whole thing down properly, because I have summarised it for forty-seven years and I notice that the summary has got shorter each time.

His office was the corner one with the two windows and a filing cabinet that would not shut, and he had a photograph of a boat, and he did not own a boat. He stood up when I came in, which he did for everybody, and then he did not know whether to sit down again, and he half sat and then stood up properly and came round and shut the door, and then went back and sat, and the whole business took about eleven seconds and neither of us said anything during it.

He had the tie clip. He had a biro in his top pocket that had gone.

He said, 'You'll have heard about Howard.'

I said I had.

He said, 'Well.' And then he said, 'It's not — ' and stopped, and started again.

What he said next I have never been able to get into one sentence, although I have tried, and the version I have been giving people for years is one sentence, and the finding of this morning is that the one-sentence version is a thing I made afterwards because it is better and because it is worse.

What he actually said, over about a minute and a half, with two long pauses in it and a bit in the middle about the reorganisation that I could not follow and do not think he could either, was this. That Howard was not the sharper of the two of us and everybody knew that. That there was a — and here he stopped for a while — that people knew where they were with Howard. That when Howard said a thing you knew what he thought about it, and that this mattered more in that post than it ought to and he was not going to pretend otherwise. That he had gone back and forth. That he had gone back and forth a good deal.

And then he said, 'You're a very difficult person to be certain about, and I don't know that that's your fault.'

He looked at the desk when he said it. Not at me.

I let a beat go, because a beat is what makes it land as generosity rather than as agreement, and then I said, 'No, I think that's fair.'

He looked up. He was so relieved that I could see it happen in his shoulders. He said something about how he'd wanted me to hear it from him, and I said something about how I appreciated that, and we did about four more minutes on the reorganisation, and he told me the thing about his daughter's flat, and then I said I'd better let him get on, which is a phrase I have used perhaps a thousand times in my life to end conversations that were going perfectly well.

I went back to my desk. There was a set of tables on it that I had been working through since the Friday. I did the rest of them that afternoon and I found two transcription errors in them, one of which mattered, and I remember the afternoon as a good one.

---

I have gone over that minute and a half more than any other minute of my life, including a rather more famous one, and I have come round slowly, and with a certain amount of resistance, to the view that Frank Loder was simply correct.

Nobody did know where they were with me. That was not an accident and it was not something that was done to me. It had taken twenty-six years, which is to say all of them, of continuous and rather skilful effort, beginning in a kitchen at Cliff Rise, and it had worked completely, and on a Tuesday afternoon in 1979 it cost me a job I would have been substantially better at than the man who got it.

I said I thought that was fair. And it was.

Frank Loder wrote to me once, eight years on, in 1987, when the liver work reached the trade press with my name in it somewhere. Two lines: *Saw the papers. Knew I was right.* No signature necessary; the handwriting was the office. I have thought better of him for it and worse, in strict rotation, ever since, and the letter is in the roof with everything else, and it is the only item up there I can quote.

---

*Howard rang me in 2011, out of nowhere, to say he had been thinking of me and that I had been the making of him, and that he had never forgotten what I did for him in that first year. I could not remember doing anything for him in that first year. I still cannot. I said of course, and I said it had been a pleasure, and we talked about his knees.*

---

He looked at the desk and not at me. I have been trying all morning to think when anybody did that to me again and it took until the second cup of tea, and the answer is forty-five years later, in a doorway, at twenty past six in the morning, and it was a young man apologising for the hour, and I was holding a basket of cutlery.

That is a long way from Nottingham and I have got there out of order.

She says she will move it.

### Four

Christine came into my room in the November of 1972 because a man called Tony had said something to her at a bus stop, and she needed to tell somebody about it immediately, and my door was the nearest one that opened.

She was loud. That is the first thing and it is not a criticism. She had a laugh you could locate from the other end of a corridor and she used it constantly and at her own expense, and she said whatever had arrived in her head about ninety seconds before a more careful person would have decided against it. She once told a professor of geomorphology, at a departmental thing, that his tie was a mistake and that somebody should have said. He agreed with her. People agreed with Christine.

She talked for four hours that first evening. Tony, and then Tony's friend, and then her mother, and then a thing that had happened at her school in 1969 that she had never told anyone, and which I still know and which is not going in this book.

I said a dozen sentences at the most and nearly all of them were questions.

At about two in the morning she said, 'God, I've talked at you all night, you must think I'm awful,' and I said something that made her laugh, and she went to bed, and I lay in mine and thought that I had never had a better evening.

That is not irony. I am being careful here, because it would be very easy to make this into a story about a woman who was used as a wall, and it was not that. I was not enduring it. I had found, at nineteen, the exact arrangement I would want for the rest of my life: total intimacy, in one direction, with somebody I loved.

Fifty-four years. She has never once asked me a question I could not answer in a sentence and a half, and it has never occurred to her that this is unusual, and I have taken enormous care that it should not.

---

She married Alec Wass in the September of 1977 and I was not a bridesmaid because she did not have any, on principle, which was very Christine, and I did the reading.

Alec taught woodwork and later design technology at a comprehensive in Nottingham. He was a quiet, extremely funny man of the kind who says four things an evening and you remember three of them, and he was besotted with her for forty-two years in a way that everybody who met them noticed within about ten minutes. They had two boys.

I want to put down here, because it will not fit anywhere else, that I liked him very much and that we never once had a conversation of any consequence, in forty-two years, and that when he died I found I had no idea what he thought about anything except her.

He was not a saint, since the account is being settled. He sulked, on a timetable, about two days a month, monumentally, over nothing that could ever be named, and the household ran around the sulk the way traffic runs around roadworks, and Christine called it his weather, and it was the only thing she ever lied to herself about in the whole time I knew them.

And she paints. I should have put that in long before the illness took all the space, because it is the thing about Christine that is nobody's wife and nobody's carer: enormous wet skies over the Trent valley, done at the kitchen table at a scale the kitchen table cannot really support, hung about the house unframed with drawing pins. They are not good. She knows they are not good. She does them anyway, at full size, in front of everybody, signed, and I have envied her that more than I have ever envied anybody their happiness.

---

In 1994 he began dropping things.

I am going to compress twenty-five years now and I am aware that compressing it is the whole problem with accounts of this kind, because the compression is exactly what the people living inside it do not get.

It was progressive and it was neurological and over twenty-five years it was given two names, the second when the first stopped fitting, and Christine never liked saying either of them, and I said them in my head every time she did not. It went slowly. That is the thing nobody understands who has not been near one. It went so slowly that for the first six years you could believe it was not really happening, and then there was a decade in the middle where he was still entirely himself and could not do up a button, and then there were about eight years that I am not going to describe, and then he died in the February of 2019, at home, with her there, which she had promised him and which cost her more than anything else in her life.

Thirteen months later the country shut its doors, and she said to me on the telephone, in the flat voice she used for about a year, that he had timed it kindly. It is the only bitter thing I have ever heard her say and she has never said it again.

The boys were good. That needs saying, because the version where the children vanish is a common one and it is not this one.

The Hamburg one is an engineer and rings her on Tuesdays still, out of a habit that has outlived its reason. The other is in Bristol and does something with land drainage that Martin, alone of everybody, finds genuinely interesting, and the two of them can do forty minutes on soakaways, and Christine and I have sat through it in that kitchen like two women at the opera, moved by different passages. They were good and they lived in Bristol and in Hamburg and they came when they could, and what she needed was not four visits a year.

---

What I did.

I have been trying for two days to work out how to set this down without it becoming a woman describing her own kindness, and I have not solved it, so I am simply going to list it and stop.

Money, from 1997. It began as four hundred pounds and by the end it was rather more than that a month, and it went in as a standing order from an account she does not know exists, described on her statement as a payment from a firm in Grantham that has never traded. She believes it is a small annuity of Alec's mother's. She has told me about it, twice, with pleasure. It is the single longest-running lie of my life and I have no intention of correcting it.

I learned to lift, as well. There is a course, half a day, a district nurse with a doll and a rubber mat, and Christine and I did it together in a church hall in 1999, two women of a certain age being taught the counting — one, two, three, and up, always on the three — and the skill is entirely in the counting, and I remember thinking, on the mat, that it was the only arithmetic in my life that had ever held another person, and I have revised that opinion since, in this book, downward.

Forms. Christine cannot do forms. It is not stupidity, she has a first, it is that a form asks you to describe the worst thing about your life in the third person and she cannot make her hand do it. So I did them. All of them, for twenty-two years, and I got very good at it.

The forms had a language and I became native in it. You learn that *can you walk fifty metres* does not mean can you, it means can you reliably, repeatedly, safely, and to what standard, and that the distance between *cannot* and *can only with difficulty* is the distance between two different tribunals. I kept a folder of phrasings that had worked, and revised it the way other women revise recipes, and I have wondered since whether the assessors on the other side kept folders of their own, of phrasings that had let them refuse, and I expect they did, and I expect somebody trained them, and I have been the somebody in other buildings, and there is no bottom to this paragraph, so I will stop it here.

In 2003 I did an appeal against a benefits decision that had been made on the basis of a fifteen-minute assessment by a woman who had asked Alec to walk to a chair.

I took the whole of an autumn over it. I got the assessment guidance and I got the case law and I built the whole thing out of their own criteria, in their own language, and I found that the descriptor they had applied could not survive their own definition of variability, and I wrote nine pages, and I have never in my life produced anything better. Not at Cattermole and not afterwards and not in the building near Victoria.

It was the finest piece of work I have ever done and it exists in a filing cabinet somewhere in the Midlands or does not exist at all, and it is signed by Christine Wass, and the tribunal allowed it in under forty minutes.

She went to the hearing alone, in her good coat, with the nine pages in a plastic wallet, and I sat that afternoon in my office above the river doing other people's signals with the telephone turned round to face me, and at ten past four it rang, and she said, 'Alison.' Just that. And then she cried for a while, expensively, down a payphone, and I stood at the window with the receiver and watched the river go out, and it remains the entire difference between us that she has never once understood why I would not let it be my victory, and I have never been able to explain that it was the only kind I could digest.

I would do that again tomorrow. I would do it instead of most of the rest of it.

And driving. That is really the whole of it. From about 1998 I went over on a Tuesday when I could and later on a Tuesday and a Friday, and it was fifty-one minutes from the house in the constituency, and considerably more from anywhere else we ever lived, and I made that drive twelve hundred times or thereabouts, and for a great many of those I did nothing when I arrived except sit in a kitchen while somebody who was very tired talked.

Here is one. I have picked it the way you pick a specimen, for being ordinary, and it is the Tuesday I would keep if I could keep one.

March of 2004, because there was snow that would not settle. I got there at ten past two. Christine had the kettle on before my coat was off, which was the constant, and the kitchen smelled of toast and of the ointment, which I had stopped noticing years before and noticed that day because of the cold.

Alec was at the table. He was in the long middle of it then, entirely himself and unable to do up a button, and in front of him was a radio he was mending, or was being allowed to mend, because mending it was a thing his hands still mostly believed they could do. He said, 'Alison. They've sent you again.' He said a version of that every Tuesday for about six years and it was funny every Tuesday, which is a thing I cannot explain to you about Alec except to say that he rationed himself, one a visit, and made it new with his eyes.

Christine talked. The boys, the wheelchair services, her mother's old neighbour, a woman at the surgery who had been short with her and whom Christine had resolved to forgive, at length. I said the things I said. At about three Alec got the back off the radio and held up the screwdriver to show me, and Christine, without turning from the sink and without stopping her sentence, reached behind her and moved his tea further from his elbow.

At four the telephone went and it was the Hamburg boy, and she took it in the hall with the door open, and Alec listened to her side of it with the whole of his face, and at one point said to me, quietly, 'He rings Tuesdays because he knows you're here. Company for her after.' Which I had never once worked out in six years of Tuesdays, and a man who could not do up a button had.

And at leaving, always the same. Christine came out to the car coatless whatever the weather, and stood at the window, and said one more thing — every visit, the true sentence saved for the gravel — and that Tuesday it was: 'He showed you the radio, didn't he. He saves you the radio.' And I drove home in the not-settling snow and bought chips at the place after the roundabout and ate them in the lay-by with the engine off, and I want it recorded that I was not unhappy in that lay-by. Not once, in all those years. It was the other thing, the one I have never had a word for, that comes when you have been of use and nobody has seen you be it.

Alec finished the radio in the June. It worked. Nobody in that house ever had the heart to tell him it had worked before.

---

I never told Martin what the money was.

He knew there was money. He is not a fool and we had a joint arrangement and he could see a standing order. He asked once, in about 1999, and I said it was for Christine, and he said, 'Right,' and that was the entire conversation, and it has never been raised again in twenty-seven years.

I did not tell him what it had grown to. There was no reason to conceal it. He would have said yes. He would have said yes cheerfully and immediately and probably suggested increasing it, and I have thought about this a great deal in the last fortnight and I cannot produce a single reason why I kept the figure to myself except that keeping things to myself is what I do, and that a thing shared is a thing that can be discussed.

---

In the spring of 1997, which was the last spring of the other business and the worst of it, I drove over on a Tuesday in a condition I am not going to describe here, because it belongs in a later chapter, and it will get one.

I sat in the kitchen for two hours and forty minutes. Alec was upstairs. Christine talked about a wheelchair services appointment and about her mother and about whether the boys were happy, and cried twice, and I said the right things, and I did the washing-up because she does not see it, and I drove home.

She has no idea. Not that I was in trouble, and not what the trouble was. It did not come up because I did not bring it up, and I would like to be clear that she would have been extraordinary if I had. She would have been better at it than anybody. She would have dropped everything.

That afternoon comes back about once a year and has done for twenty-nine, and I have never got any further with it than this: I could have had that. It was ninety pence and one sentence away. And I preferred the kitchen the way it was.

---

Martin said something about her in 2006.

We had been over there for a lunch, one of about four occasions in the whole time when the four of us were in a room, and on the way back he was quiet for a while and then he said, in the voice he uses when he has been turning something over rather than when he is being clever, that he thought Christine minded about me.

I asked what he meant.

He said he did not think she liked being the one who was helped. He said it was not resentment exactly, and that she was not aware of it, and that he had watched her be funny about the money for about ten minutes at the table and that funny was where she was putting it. And then he said that people can love you very much and still be waiting for you to need something, and that if the waiting goes on for thirty years it turns into something else, and that he was probably wrong.

I said I thought he was probably wrong.

He said, 'Yes, probably,' in a way that meant he was not going to have an argument about it, and we talked about something else, and that was 2006, and I have not thought about it in any serious way since.

I am thinking about it now.

Because he was not wrong, and I have known he was not wrong for twenty years in the way you know things you have declined to open. He saw it in ten minutes at a lunch table. I have been going over there since 1998 and I never saw it once, and I am the one who is supposed to be able to see.

And the reason I never saw it is not complicated and I have got to it in about ninety seconds this morning, which is the fastest I have ever got to anything in this room. If Christine minded being helped, then the arrangement was not a gift. It was a transaction in which I was buying something, and the thing I was buying was fifty-four years of a friendship in which I never had to say anything.

I do not think that is the whole of it. I have loved her since 1972 and I would have done every single one of those drives for nothing.

But it is not none of it either, and I have never got the proportion right, and I shall not get it right in the next four months.

---

She comes here now.

That is the thing I did not plan for. Since March she has been driving over, fifty-one minutes, on a Tuesday and sometimes a Friday, and she sits in the chair by the window and she talks, and she brings things I cannot eat.

She is seventy-three and she has been on her own for seven years and her knees are bad and she should not be doing that road in the dark.

And I have discovered that I cannot bear it. Not the fact of it. The particular shape of it, which is that she asked me last week, straight out, whether I was frightened, and looked at me while she waited.

I said something. I do not remember what I said and I have not asked Josie to look, and she asked again the following Tuesday, in almost exactly the same words, which she has never done in fifty-four years about anything.

---

*Josie read part of this back on Thursday afternoon and Martin was in the doorway and heard some of it, and afterwards, in the kitchen, he said: 'I didn't know about the tribunal.' And then, after a bit: 'Nineteen years.' And then he made the tea.*

### Five

Market Towers stood on Nine Elms Lane and was two towers of brown glass put up in 1974 by somebody who has never been made to account for it. Ours was the taller one. The lifts were a subject in themselves. There was a smell in the stairwell that everybody attributed to the flower market and which was not the flower market.

I went there in the September of 1980 and I stayed twenty-five years, through two changes of name and one change of building, and after Cattermole I never worked anywhere else.

On my first morning the lifts were out, and I climbed with a man from establishments who gave me the building's three rules unprompted, between floors, breathing hard: never trust the top-floor kettle, never say the word *drug* to the press office, and never let Maureen Skitt hear you call a card a form. I kept all three for twenty-five years, and I have received far worse inductions with far better funding.

The commissionaire was a Mr Odubote who had taught chemistry in Ibadan and who called every woman in that building Doctor, on the grounds, he told me once, that he had been wrong the other way round twice in his first month and had decided never to be wrong that way again. He was there before me every morning for seventeen years. I never once beat him in and I stopped trying in about 1984.

On his last morning, in 1997, he stood at the door and shook hands with the building as it arrived, one by one, which took until ten, and management let it, because there are mornings when management can read a room. When I reached the front he said, 'Doctor,' and I said, 'Mr Odubote,' and he held my hand a moment longer than the man before me and said, 'You were the one it was true about,' and was gone by the time I had anything, which took years.

Cyril Bett ran the post room and knew, of the four hundred companies on the register, which ones sent things second class.

---

I want to put the work down properly, because nobody ever does. The books about my husband's period contain a sentence about me and the sentence is that I had a background in medicines regulation. One of them says pharmaceuticals, which is a different thing, and which I have never bothered to correct.

Here is what it actually was.

A doctor in Carlisle sees a patient with a rash. He thinks the rash might be the tablets. He is not sure, and he has eleven minutes, and there is a yellow card in the back of the prescribing book, and he fills it in, or he does not.

That is the whole system. It has been the whole system since 1964, since thalidomide, which is the event standing behind every desk in that building the way the war stood behind my grandmother. A voluntary card, filled in by a tired person who thinks something might be connected to something.

Some of them wrote in the margins. That was my favourite discovery of the early years and nobody had told me to expect it. The card has boxes, and the boxes are the system, and around the boxes, in the handwriting of exhausted people, was the country: *lovely woman, very anxious*; *third one I've seen this month — is it me?*; *she thinks it's the tablets, and she may be right*. None of it could be counted. All of it was information. There was a debate, about every four years, on whether the margins should be captured somehow, and it ended the same way every time, on cost, and the margins went on being written and going nowhere, like most of what people know.

The cards came to us. Somebody opened them. Somebody read the doctor's handwriting and decided what he meant. Somebody assigned the reaction a term from a dictionary of permitted terms, because you cannot count things that are described in eleven thousand different ways, and so a woman in Carlisle with a rash becomes a code, and from that moment onwards she is not a woman with a rash.

That is not a criticism. It is the only way it can be done. I should be clear about that, because I spent twenty-five years doing it, and because everything that went wrong later went wrong inside a system that was correctly designed.

Then the counting. How many of this code, for this drug, against how many you would expect, given how many people take it and how many people get that thing anyway.

Then the number.

There is always a number. There has to be. You cannot have a human being look at everything, so you set a level at which a human being looks, and below the level nobody looks, and the level is chosen by reasonable people for defensible reasons.

It moved twice while I was there. On both occasions the discussion was about resource. On both occasions the discussion was entirely honest.

There is one more number of this kind in this book. It was set further up, by people I never met, and we will come to it.

---

Maureen Skitt ran the coding on the fourth floor and had been there since 1969.

She kept a cardigan on the back of her chair in all weathers and a biscuit tin with a picture of Whitby on it, and she could tell you without looking anything up which firms filled their cards in properly and which sent you three lines of nothing.

I sat with her for two days in my first fortnight. It was not required and I asked for it, and she was pleased and did not say so.

She held up a card from a general practitioner in Motherwell.

'Now. This one. He says nausea. He does not mean nausea. He always says nausea and he means the other thing, and I know that because I rang him in 1974 and asked him.'

I said, was that written down anywhere.

She said, 'It's written down here,' and tapped the side of her head, and laughed, because in 1980 that was a joke and not yet a description of a structural risk.

She retired in the March of 1991. They gave her a decanter and a card went round two floors. About eight months later a query came up about how a particular set of reports from 1977 had been classified, and there was no answer anywhere in the building, because the answer had been Maureen, and Maureen was in Poole.

Somebody suggested ringing her. Somebody else said you could not really do that. And that was the end of it, and the reports stayed as they were, and they are as they are now.

Every system of that kind, however well built, is at some point standing on one person who knows a thing that is written down nowhere. It does not know which person, or which thing, until they have gone.

The committee met on the top floor at a long table that had come from somewhere grander, and it was served coffee in green cups by a man called Wesley, whose other duty was the post, and who timed the refills to the agenda, and who was therefore, I eventually understood, reading the agenda, and was the only person on that floor doing so for pleasure. There was a professor of cardiology who fell asleep once a meeting, reliably, for four or five minutes, and woke always at the item that mattered, and I never established the mechanism, and Rosalind said it was thirty years of ward rounds and to leave it alone.

---

*She asked me this morning whether I liked it. Not whether I was good at it. She has asked twice now, in two different ways, and both times I have answered the other question, and I did it again this morning and heard myself do it.*

---

Rosalind Teague came across from a chair in epidemiology in 1983 and ran the section for seventeen years.

She was eleven years older than me. She had done medicine first and had not enjoyed patients. She kept a pencil in her hand through every meeting, and never wrote with it; it was an instrument of tempo, laid down for emphasis, raised for doubt, and the whole section could read her pencil by 1985, and she knew we could, and used it accordingly, and I have run rooms with a teaspoon on the same principle. She was not warm. She did not remember your children's names and would not have pretended to. What she had instead was that she was right, at speed, in front of people, about difficult things, and did not require you to admire her for it.

In the October of 1985 a company came in about a product I am not going to name, because two of the people involved have grandchildren.

There were three of them. The senior was a Dr Charteris, who had a good suit and the habit of saying 'as I'm sure you're aware' in front of things one was not aware of. They had brought a submission of about ninety pages arguing that a cluster of reports could be explained by a change in the diagnostic criteria used in secondary care.

There were five of us. I was there to hand over the tables.

Rosalind let him talk for eleven minutes. She did not interrupt once. She made a note twice.

Then she said, 'Can I take you to your table four.'

'Of course.'

'Your denominator here is patient-years.'

'It is.'

'And on table six it's patients.'

There was a pause of about two seconds. He said the two tables were addressing different questions.

'They are. But your conclusion on page sixty-one uses them as though they were the same, and if you run patient-years throughout, your reporting rate goes up by a factor of about two and a half, and I think that puts it above the line you have drawn yourself on page sixty-three. Would you like a minute with that?'

He said he would want to take it away.

'Do. And when it comes back — if the criteria explanation is right, the effect should be strongest in the centres that changed their criteria and absent in the ones that didn't. I don't think you've looked at that, because I don't think you have the centre-level data. Have you the centre-level data?'

He said they would look at what was available.

She said, 'Right.' And then, to the room: 'Does anybody want a biscuit? There are some in the cupboard and they'll go soft.'

Four minutes. She did not raise her voice at any point, and she was not, in any way I could identify, enjoying herself.

In the lift afterwards Howard said, 'God.'

I said nothing at all, because what I was doing was going back over it to see whether I would have got there, and the answer was that I would have got there in about a day and a half, on paper, alone, and would then have written it down and given it to somebody else to say out loud.

Dr Charteris, for completeness, came back in the January with the centre-level data, which they did have after all, and it did not help him, and the product was restricted in the spring. At the door, leaving, he shook Rosalind's hand and said, 'It's a pleasure to lose to you, Professor,' which was the most honest sentence anybody from that side said in that room in twenty-five years, and she said, 'It shouldn't be a pleasure, Dr Charteris,' and asked Wesley whether there was more coffee.

---

I was very happy there.

The first four years I did nothing but sit with the data, in green folders and then on a terminal that took forty minutes to produce anything and was still a miracle, and I got back to where I had been in the room over the boiler at Nottingham, except that now the numbers were people who had actually taken something.

A line listing at scale has a texture. It came on green-bar paper then, folded on itself like bedding, and it smelled of the print room, and you learned to read it at a slight angle with the top sheet turned back, and the sound of a floor of people doing that — the soft turn, the pause, the turn — was the sound of my thirties, and I would have it again over most music.

There were Saturdays, in the good years, when I came in without being asked. The building nearly empty, Mr Odubote at the desk with his crossword, the lift to myself, and I would put three hours into something nobody had commissioned, for nobody, invoiced to nobody, and those hours are, pound for pound, the happiest of my professional life, and I told nobody about them either.

There is a thing that happens perhaps five times in a career. You are looking at a column and there is nothing in it, and you know there is nothing in it, and then something turns over. Not a discovery. A wrongness.

In the spring of 1986 I found one.

Eight reports of a liver reaction on a drug called Trevanide, for which there was no reason to expect a liver reaction. Seven of the eight were women. Six of the seven were over sixty-five.

It took me twelve days to be sure. On the twelfth day I did the thing I have been doing since I was fifteen, which is that I made it smaller before I carried it anywhere.

I went in to Rosalind with four pages and said that there was possibly something, and that it might be nothing.

She read it standing up. Then she sat down, which took about three seconds and which I remember more exactly than anything anybody has ever said to me.

'Why did you say it might be nothing?'

I said eight was a small number.

'Eight is a small number. Seven out of eight in women when the prescribing is fifty-five per cent men is not a small number. You know that. Why did you say it might be nothing?'

I did not have an answer. I have had forty years and I have several now, and I did not have one then.

She said, 'Do it properly, bring it to me on Friday, and don't apologise for it in the first paragraph.'

She took it to the committee in the June and put my name on it, twice, out loud, when she could perfectly well have carried it herself.

I was not in the room. Analysts waited in the corridor in those days, in case of questions, on two chairs under a print of a bridge, and the doors were heavy but not heavy enough, and I heard my own name come through the oak twice, in her voice, in the cadence that means a name is being attached to something, and then the long murmur a committee makes when it is agreeing, which is lower than the murmur it makes when it is not, and you learn the difference in one afternoon and keep it for life. Wesley came out with the tray and told me before anybody official did. He said, 'They've taken it. All of it.' And then, professionally: 'He nodded off after.' In the corridor afterwards she said, 'That was a good piece of work,' and did not elaborate. I have got more out of those five words in forty years than out of every other compliment I have received put together.

All of that should be on the page now, before anything else, because of what comes later. It would be extremely convenient for me if Rosalind Teague had been a monster. She was the best of us.

---

Howard Slee came across from Cattermole in 1984 and told everybody within a fortnight that I had taught him everything he knew, and did it in a way that made people look at me kindly for about six months.

I have never established whether he knew what he was doing. I have concluded, slowly, that he did not, and that this is exactly why it worked, and that there is an entire category of person who gets on in life by being unable to calculate.

He married a woman called Jan who did something with horses. They had us to dinner in Beckenham in 1987 and Jan said over the pudding, 'Alison, you must find him infuriating,' meaning Howard, and everybody laughed, and I said, 'Constantly,' and everybody laughed again, and Howard was delighted.

I did not find him infuriating. I found him restful, for the same reason I have found other things restful, and I did not work that out until about four years ago.

---

We were the Medicines Division of the Department, and then in 1989 an executive agency with a chief executive and a logo, and in 2003 we merged with the devices people and became something else again. Each change involved a quantity of paper, a new sign, a reception with warm wine, and about four months in which everybody was slightly worse at their job.

I still have the mug from 1989. It has a lion on it. I do not know why there is a lion.

The chief executive we were given wore a lapel pin of the new logo and said 'customer' in his first address, meaning the companies, and a noise went through that room which I have only otherwise heard at a wedding when the best man has misjudged something. He learned. To be fair to him, which I am trying to be, he learned quickly, and he protected the work for nine years, and when he left, the customers gave him a dinner, which everybody understood, and only Cyril said out loud.

---

By 1990 I was running the signal work. Not the section. Rosalind ran the section and would run it for another decade. I ran the part that mattered, which is a distinction I made at the time with a certain amount of quiet satisfaction and which I would now put rather differently.

I was thirty-seven. I was better at that work than anybody in the building, including her, and the way I knew it was that I could tell you what was going to be a signal about four months before the arithmetic could.

I never once said so.

There was a promotion board in the autumn of 1993 which I did not go for.

I have given four accounts of that over the years, to four different people. That I did not want to manage. That the timing was wrong because of my mother. That I did not want to be taken off the data. That I was perfectly happy where I was.

All four are true. None of them is the reason. The reason is that the board involved a presentation to eleven people, and I had worked out, some time in about 1974, that I would never stand up in front of eleven people and tell them what I could do.

I told Rosalind I was happy where I was.

She had a pencil in her hand and she put it down, which she did not usually do, and she looked at me for slightly too long.

'Right.' And then, after a moment: 'Well. You know where I am.'

I said thank you.

I have wondered since whether that was the moment she decided something about me. There is no evidence for it. There is a look, across a desk, in 1993, and thirty-three years in which to make it mean whatever I have needed it to mean on the day.

---

The building was condemned in the end, which surprised nobody, although it took until after the merger for anybody to act on it. We went at last to Buckingham Palace Road, to rooms with windows that opened and a canteen people actually used, and everybody said it was better and everybody meant it, and I had about eighteen months of it before I left.

On the last afternoon at Nine Elms somebody found a filing cabinet that nobody had a key for, and Cyril Bett opened it with a screwdriver, and it held four boxes of yellow cards from 1971 that had never been entered.

There was a short discussion in the corridor about what to do. Somebody said it was thirty years ago. Somebody else said that was not the point. And they were entered, over about three weeks, by two people on overtime, and nothing came of it.

I read some of them before they went for entry. I was not required to; I went down on the second evening because I could not stay away, and I sat with a box of 1971 for an hour, and the handwriting was the same handwriting, and the margins were the same margins, and on one card, against a rash in a child, a doctor long retired or dead had written: *mother certain it's the syrup. Mothers usually right.* Nineteen seventy-one. It had waited in a locked drawer through five governments to be counted, and when it was counted it counted for nothing, because the syrup was long gone, and I have never known whether the whole afternoon is a story about a system that works.

I have thought about that cabinet perhaps twice a year for more than twenty years. Josie says it is a good place to end a chapter and I have said she can have it.

### Six

I met my husband at a party in Nottingham in the March of 1982, at Christine's, in a kitchen, which I notice.

It was a housewarming and there were about thirty people and the flat was over a carpet showroom on the Mansfield Road, and Christine had made a chilli in a quantity that suggested a misunderstanding about how many people had said yes.

He was thirty-four. He was not good-looking. There are photographs and people are kind about them, and the truth is that he had a large plain face, and stood badly, and was going at the front, and what he had instead was that when he came into a room the temperature of it altered slightly in his favour within about ninety seconds and nobody could tell you how.

He had come with somebody else. I want to be exact about this because it has been reported wrongly twice: he came with a woman called Judith who worked in the same building as him and who was not his girlfriend and who married a dentist the following year.

He was leaning against the cooker because it was the only warm thing in the flat. I had gone into the kitchen to get away from a man who was explaining Poland to me.

He said, 'You're Alison. Chris says you do something at the Ministry.'

I said I worked in medicines regulation.

That is the sentence. I used it for twenty-five years and it is designed to close a conversation, and it worked in London a hundred and forty-odd times, and I had got so used to it working that I had stopped listening for what came back.

He said, 'What, actually?'

I said, 'Sorry?'

He said, 'What do you actually do. In the day. Because I've no idea what that would be.'

So I told him. I told him about the yellow card and the doctor in Carlisle with eleven minutes. I told him that the woman with the rash becomes a code and stops being a woman with a rash. I told him about the number, and about what sits underneath the number, and I heard myself explaining, at a party, on a Saturday, the thing I had never once explained to my mother.

He asked three questions. The first was how many cards. The second was whether the doctors got paid for them, which is a good question and the answer is no. The third was, 'So who decides what the number is?'

I said a committee.

He said, 'Yes, but who?'

I said that was rather the point.

He laughed at that. Not politely. He put his head back and laughed and said, 'God, that's terrifying,' and I said, 'It is, rather,' and I had been talking to him for twenty-five minutes.

Then a man called Ivor Pask came in for a bottle opener, and Martin turned and said, 'Ivor. Now then. Did you sort it out with the flue?' and Ivor Pask told him about the flue for six or seven minutes, and Martin listened to that with exactly the attention he had given me, and asked two questions about it, and the second one was good.

Ivor Pask, for completeness, since this book settles accounts: Martin was at his funeral in 2009, one of about a dozen there, on no connection anybody could name beyond a bottle opener and a flue in 1982, and that is my husband entire, in a sentence I have been circling since the yellow house. He attends.

I stood there with my glass and I registered it and I put it away.

He worked for the county council in social services and had done since 1971, having done a year of teacher training and hated it.

He was, by 1982, running the part that dealt with residential care for the elderly, which nobody wanted and which he had asked for. His mother, Doreen, had been in a home in Sutton-in-Ashfield for three years by then, and it was not a good one, and he had got her moved, and the process of getting her moved had taken him eleven months and had turned him, permanently, into a man with a subject.

I met her at the home first, the good one, the one it had taken him a year to win, and the meeting was conducted on her ground and her terms, in the day room, with a tin of shortbread deployed like regalia, and half the residents found reasons to pass the door, because word had gone round that Martin's young lady was in, and I was thirty and had been called many things and never that, and Doreen watched me notice all of it and offered me the tin last, after two passers-by, to see what I would do with the order of precedence, and I took the plainest one, and we understood each other from there.

I met Doreen five times before she died. She was a small woman with a very direct way of looking at you and she said to me on the second occasion, 'He's not clever, you know. He's just interested.' She said it fondly. I thought at the time that it was a slightly cruel thing to say about your son to a woman he was seeing.

It has taken me forty years to work out that it was neither cruel nor foolish and that it was the single most accurate sentence anybody has ever said about Martin, and that his mother, who left school at fourteen, had got there in one go.

The second time I met her she made me move my chair. I had sat where the light was behind me, which is where I sit, and she waved a hand and said, 'Come round here where I can see what you're not saying,' and Martin laughed, and I moved, and we looked at each other, Doreen and I, for a moment longer than the joke required, and something was established that neither of us ever afterwards referred to. Before the wedding she sent me a card, separate from the official one. It said: *He needs telling things twice. The second time, mean it less.* I have followed that instruction for forty-two years and it works, and I have never told anybody I was given it.

There was also a brother, Neil, who was two years younger and who is going to come into this book once, much later, and about whom I will say nothing here.

We were together about eighteen months before there was any question of anything.

He was in Nottingham and I was in London and it was the train, on Fridays, alternately, for a year and a half, and we wrote letters, which sounds romantic and was mostly administrative.

*Josie asked whether I still have them and I said no and then said I would look, which was a way of ending the question. They are in the roof. I have not looked and I do not intend to and I have not told her that.*

I will say one thing about the letters, from memory, and then leave them up there. His were lists, mostly. Things seen, people met, a council meeting rendered as a cast of characters. Mine were replies. If you had only the envelopes you would say two people equally engaged; if you read them you would find a man handing over his week entire, and a woman returning it improved, with nothing of her own enclosed, and neither correspondent ever noticed, for eighteen months, and one of them still hasn't.

We married in the October of 1984 at the register office on Shakespeare Street and there were eleven people.

My parents. My brother Colin, who was twenty-three and wore a suit of my father's. Christine and Alec, who had been married seven years by then and were already faintly proprietorial about the whole business. Doreen, in a wheelchair, furious about the wheelchair. Neil. A man called Terry Isard from Martin's office who was there because Martin could not work out how to not invite him. And a woman called Bridget whose surname I have genuinely forgotten and who I believe was a cousin of somebody.

That morning, at my parents' house, my mother came into the back bedroom while I was doing my hair, straightened the bedspread I had already straightened, and said, 'Well.' Then she went down and made the men their breakfast. That is the entire mother-of-the-bride record, and I have envied women their fuller versions, and doubted I could have stood one, in about equal measure.

We had lunch afterwards at the Marchmont, which was a hotel on the ring road and which is flats now. It did a set lunch: soup, chicken, a pudding with a name longer than the pudding. There were speeches, badly, and Alec did four sentences that were the funniest thing anybody said all day, and my father cried, which nobody expected including my mother.

Terry Isard, whom Martin could not work out how to not invite, turned out to be the find of the day, because he had been a wedding photographer in a former life he had mentioned to nobody, and he took, on Alec's camera, the only photographs from that day anyone has ever framed, including one of my mother laughing — actually laughing, head back — at something Doreen said that neither of them would ever repeat, and I have looked at that photograph for forty years the way other people look at photographs of the sea.

My mother said to me in the ladies, adjusting something, 'Well, he's very sound.' Which in her vocabulary was not damning and was not praise and was, I now think, simply the truth, and which took her four words.

Bridget, the cousin of somebody, caught the half-past-four train and sent a tea towel from Llandudno the following week with a note that said *to the pair of you, every happiness, B*, and we never established whose cousin she was, and the tea towel outlasted three kitchens, because Martin would not have it thrown out, on the grounds, he said, that you don't discard a witness.

Then we had three days in Wensleydale, because the diary of a county social services department does not recognise weddings. We stayed at a farm that did rooms, under a Mrs Askey, who served breakfast at one long table and ran it like a small assembly, and the only other guests were a couple from Redcar up for their ruby anniversary who held hands between the courses, and Mrs Askey referred to the four of us collectively as 'my couples' and to the weather as though it reported to her. On the second afternoon Martin helped Mr Askey move a chest freezer, voluntarily, gladly, for two hours, and came back with the entire history of the farm and an opinion about milk quotas that he held for the rest of his life. In the visitors' book, leaving, he wrote four lines. I wrote 'lovely', and then my new name, for the first time anywhere, and stood looking at it in the hall with my case in my hand, and Mrs Askey, passing, looked over my shoulder and said, 'It'll write easier by Christmas,' which remains the wisest thing anybody said to me about marriage, and she went on through with the toast rack.

The first house was on a quiet road in Cheam with a garden that the previous owner, a widower going to his daughter's, had loved past the point of sense, and he walked Martin round it for an hour on completion day, handing over the peonies by name, and left him the tools, including a fork with a mended handle that Martin used for forty years and would not replace, on the usual grounds. The neighbours took us for a settled couple within a month, because Martin knew all of them within a month, and the road had opinions about us before we had unpacked the books, and the opinions were kind, and I lived on that road for twenty-two years and was happy there in my fashion.

---

Now the reasons, which I have given many times and which are all true.

He was kind. He was funny. He was extremely good company and interested in the world in a way that did not require the world to be interested in him. He wanted children and I believed I did. I was thirty-one and my mother had been running a weather system on the subject since about 1979. And I liked him enormously, which is not nothing and about which people are far too quick to be rude.

Every one of those is true and I would give them again in the same order.

What I have never managed, in forty-two years, is to produce the one underneath them.

I have come close perhaps three times, always in the car, always on the flat past Heckington where there is nothing to look at.

It has something to do with the fact that on the evening I met him he asked me a second question, and then ninety seconds later turned round and was exactly as delighted by Ivor Pask and a flue. I understood in the kitchen, at the time, without any distress at all, that the second question was not about me. It was what he did. He did it to everybody, all his life, and it was completely sincere, and it cost him nothing.

Which meant that I could be asked and never actually examined. For as long as I liked. By somebody I was fond of.

I shall not finish that thought here. I have had a good run at it and I can feel myself arranging the end of it, and I would rather leave it where it stops.

Pat Ellery arrived in the November of 1981, four months before Martin, in a church hall off the Cheam Road, at a meeting about a proposed road.

There were nine of us and a man called Mr Rudkin in the chair who had brought an agenda with six items on it for a group that had existed for eleven days. Item four was Correspondence. There had been no correspondence.

Pat had brought a flask. Not for herself. She went round with it at the interval, which there should not have been, and she got to me last and said, 'You're the one who hasn't said anything,' and poured it before I had answered.

I said I was mostly there to listen.

She said, 'That's what I thought. So which way do you actually think it should go, because Rudkin's got it wrong and nobody's going to tell him.'

I told her which way I thought it should go and why, in about ninety seconds, and it was the first properly indiscreet thing I had said to a stranger in years.

She said, 'Right.' And then she stood up and said it to the room, as her own, without a flicker, and Rudkin took it, and the meeting turned round in about four minutes.

Afterwards she said, 'Was that all right? I do that.'

The road was never built and I could not tell you whether we had anything to do with that. What we got instead was that Pat rang the following week about something else, and then the week after, and by the spring we had a lunch, and we have had four a year ever since.

The first one set the form. Fortes, on the high street, and Pat ordered for us both without consulting me — 'you'll have the soup, you look like soup today' — and I had the soup, and she was right, I was soup that day, and she talked for the length of the lunch and paid before I could reach my bag, on the grounds that I could get the next one, and the next one was in the June, and the form has never changed in forty-five years: she reads me at the door, feeds me accordingly, tells me everything, and leaves happier, and there are worse liturgies, and I have attended most of them in state.

I have said elsewhere that Pat has never asked me a question requiring an answer, and I would like to correct that now, because it is more satisfying than it is accurate.

She has asked. Repeatedly, over forty-five years, in her way, which is to talk for forty minutes and then say something like 'and what about you, are you all right,' in a tone that makes it easy to say yes.

I have always said yes. It is not her fault that the door is easy to close. She built it that way for herself, because she needs to talk and the talking is what keeps her upright, and I understood that some time in about 1983 and have made use of it ever since, and I would do it again, and I am fond of her past all reason. The forty minutes are mostly Gillian now, and have been for ten years.

---

Now the other part, which is going in, and about which Josie has been extremely professional.

The marriage was not celibate and was not unhappy and I have no complaint of any kind. It was, for the first fifteen years or so, perfectly regular and perfectly pleasant. He was affectionate. I was not cold. There was nothing wrong.

What there was not, at any point, in forty-two years, was the thing everybody else appears to be describing.

I have been listening to women talk about this since 1971. Christine at Nottingham, at length and from a height. Pat, later, in a different register. Women at work. Women at dinners for eight years, some of them very senior, after the second bottle. And for fifty-five years I have nodded and contributed and made the right noises and have not once known what any of them were talking about.

Not disapproval. I have never been prudish and I have never been frightened of it. It is simpler and much harder to say: I have never in my life been overtaken by anything. Not by a person, not by a want, not by a feeling that arrived without being sent for. I have read about it and watched it happen to everybody I love and believed all of them, and it has never come for me.

I used to think it would. Then I thought perhaps it had and I had failed to recognise it. Then I stopped thinking about it, some time in my forties, the way you stop thinking about a language you did not learn.

*Christine came to me in the summer of 1996 about a man who was not Alec. She sat in my kitchen for five hours. I talked her out of it and I was right and she has thanked me for it twice since. I had no idea what she was going through. None. I was directing traffic in a country I have never been to.*

---

Once, I tried.

It was the summer of 1986 and we were in the kitchen at Cheam and I had come back from the committee that had taken the liver work, and it had gone extremely well, and Rosalind had put my name on it twice out loud, and I was as pleased with myself as I have ever allowed myself to be.

He was doing something at the sink. He had his sleeves up. He had been in Sheffield all day about a closure and had got back at seven and was telling me about a woman who had stood up at the meeting and said something that had stopped the room.

There was a gap. I had been waiting for it since the train.

And I said, in the middle of it, 'You know, I think I could run the whole division.'

I said it lightly. I am going to be accurate about how I said it, because everything turns on that and I have had forty years to be honest. I did not say it as a declaration. I said it in the voice you use when you are offering somebody the chance to take you seriously while simultaneously handing them the means to treat it as a joke.

He said, 'Course you could.'

He said it warmly, immediately, without turning round, and he meant it absolutely. He has never in forty-two years doubted that I could do anything I said I could do.

And then he said, 'Did I tell you about Doreen's chair?'

I have been asked whether I was hurt. I was not hurt. I stood in a kitchen for about four seconds with a door open in front of me, and the door did not close, and nobody closed it, and I looked at it, and then I got the plates down.

He would have listened. I have written that sentence about Mr Cansdale in an earlier chapter and I am aware of the repetition and it is staying, because it turns out to be the shape of the whole thing. They would all have listened.

---

There was a bad patch in 1998 and I do not intend to make more of it than it was.

We were distant for the better part of a year. It was nobody's fault. He was away a great deal by then and I was not myself, and we came out of the other side of it, as people do, without ever once having a conversation about it, which I suspect is how most of them are actually got through.

That is all I want to say about that.

### Seven

We stopped taking precautions in the November of 1984, three weeks after the wedding, in the house at Cheam.

Christine's first was born in the August of 1986 and I drove down the following morning and held him in a chair by the window and he was nine days early and had a mark on his forehead that went in a fortnight.

In the March of 1987 I went to the surgery on Malden Road and saw a Dr Ferrar, who was a locum, and who said that two and a half years was not long and that I should come back at three. I came back at three. He had gone. The partner I saw instead, a Dr Prydderch, referred us both that afternoon.

Mr Padget had rooms on the first floor of a building off Weymouth Street with a lift that had a folding gate. His secretary was a Mrs Hallam, who wore reading glasses on a chain and who had a way of saying 'he's running a little behind' that I could reproduce for you now.

### Eight

Velmarid was licensed in 1997 for type 2 diabetes in patients with established cardiovascular disease.

That last clause is the whole of what follows, and anybody who is not interested in the technical part should read it once anyway, because everything I did and did not do stands on it.

The people who took Velmarid were people who already had heart disease. That is what it was for. It was given to patients whose hearts were, by definition, in trouble. So when reports began arriving of those patients having cardiac events, the reports were arriving from a population in which cardiac events were the expected background noise of their lives.

The term is confounding by indication. It means that the thing you are looking at and the reason the person was given the drug are tangled together and cannot be pulled apart by observation alone. It is not an excuse. It is a real and very common problem, and half the errors in my field in the last fifty years have been people forgetting it.

---

Rosalind assessed the first cluster in the spring of 1999.

I was in the room. There were four of us and a set of nine reports and she went through them one at a time and did not hurry, and at the end she said, 'It's the disease. I'd like somebody to tell me it isn't, and if anybody can, come and find me.'

Gerald Pyke, who was the statistician and who disagreed with her roughly once a fortnight as a matter of hygiene, said that the numbers were small enough that you could not say much either way.

She said, 'That's right. That's exactly right, and it will be true for about two years, and in two years we should look again.'

I said nothing, because I agreed with her, and because there was nothing to say. I wrote a note supporting the assessment on the twenty-second of April 1999 and that note exists and I have seen it since and it is correct.

I want that here because of what people do with hindsight. In 1999 she was right. I was right. Gerald was right about the width of it. There was no error in that room.

---

The section in 2000 was eleven of us on the ninth floor at Market Towers.

Sandra Quirk ran the diary and the leave and the room bookings and the birthdays, and had been doing it for nineteen years, and told me once that she had never in all that time understood what any of us actually did and had settled, in about 1985, on its not mattering.

When the Grade 6 was confirmed she came to my desk with the diary under her arm, as though the diary were a witness, and said, 'I've buried four reorganisations, pet. You'll be all right.' Which was not congratulation and was better than congratulation, and was, I worked out on the train home, the section being formally handed over, by its actual owner, to its nominal one.

Ken Vardy sat by the window. He was sixty-one and had come from the licensing side and had eighteen months to go and was, by then, entirely made of precedent. If you said anything to Ken he would tell you when we had last had one of those. He was not stupid and he was not lazy. He was a man who had been in a building for thirty years and had become a sort of index of it.

Nirmala Raghavan was twenty-six and had been with us fourteen months.

She read the narratives.

Everybody else read the codes, because the codes are what the system is built to be read in, and a narrative is a paragraph of a doctor's handwriting transcribed by somebody in Croydon. Nim read the narratives. It took her four times as long as it took anybody else and it made her slow on the throughput measures and Ken had mentioned it twice in her hearing.

In the February she came over to my desk with a printout and said, 'Can I show you something that's probably nothing.'

It was a Velmarid report from a hospital in Wakefield. The coded outcome was myocardial infarction. The narrative said that the patient had been readmitted twice in six weeks and that the second admission had been thought at the time to be a reaction to something and that the reporting doctor had put a question mark in brackets.

She said, 'The question mark isn't anywhere. There's no field for a question mark.'

I said that was true and that there never had been.

She said, 'Doesn't that bother you?'

I said it bothered everybody for the first year and then it stopped bothering you, and that this was probably a bad thing, and she laughed and went back to her desk.

She kept a plant on that desk, one of the kind that survive being forgotten, and a postcard of a temple, and a mug from a university whose name I would learn much later, from a departmental page, under a photograph of a woman in a green jacket. And she was the only person on that floor who rang the reporting doctors back with follow-up questions, in the evenings, on her own time, and the switchboard logged the calls, and Ken raised the logs once at a Tuesday meeting, as a question of process, and it was agreed that she would note such calls in advance in future, and she did, and there were fewer of them.

That was in the February. In the June I found the other thing.

---

It was not the number of events. The number was unremarkable and would stay unremarkable for another two years.

It was the shape of them. Across a set of reports that ought to have been randomly distributed with respect to when treatment started, they were not randomly distributed. There was a slight but persistent clustering in the first eleven to fourteen weeks.

Background disease does not know when you started your tablets.

I sat with it for nine days.

I did it in the mornings before the building filled up, which is when I did everything difficult for twenty-five years. Mr Odubote had retired in 1997 and the door was a card by then, which I have always thought a considerable loss. It beeped you in at six, the floor was dark except the security lights, and there is a particular quality to a government building at that hour, a sort of held breath with carpet, and I would get tea from the machine that only worked before eight, as though it too preferred the early shift, and sit down with the thing that was wrong, and the first morning I simply looked at the distribution for an hour the way you look at a face you cannot place.

I ran it four ways. I stratified by age band and it held. I stratified by whether the report came from primary or secondary care and it held. I looked at whether the clustering could be produced by a change in prescribing volume over the period, which would have explained it entirely, and it could not, quite. And I looked at whether it could be a reporting artefact, the thing where doctors are more alert in the first weeks of a new drug and report more of everything, and there it fell over, because on a reporting artefact you would expect the clustering to be there for all the coded outcomes and not only the cardiac ones, and I could not get the data cut finely enough to say.

Two of the four held. The two that did not were the two you would want.

On the ninth day I understood something that I do not believe anybody else in that building understood at that date, which is that this signal would never come up through the normal route, because the events were being coded to the disease, and so the reporting rate for the reaction would never rise, because there was no reaction being reported. It was all going in under the thing the patients already had.

---

What I wrote was this, and I can give it exactly, because I have had the wording for twenty-six years:

*The temporal distribution of reported events is not inconsistent with the natural history of the underlying condition, although some clustering in the early treatment period is noted. Further characterisation may be warranted if the reporting rate changes.*

Every word of that is defensible. *Not inconsistent* is doing an enormous amount and doing it legitimately, because at that date the finding was not inconsistent with anything. *May be warranted* is the standard construction. *If the reporting rate changes* is the correct trigger, and it is the trigger that failed, for the reason I had worked out on the ninth day and did not put in the paragraph.

I have shown that sentence, over the years, to two people who did not know I had written it. Both said it was a reasonable assessment. One said it was a good one.

It went into the periodic review. The periodic review went to Rosalind. Rosalind signed it. Nobody did anything, which is what the paragraph was built to produce.

The Tuesday meeting was at half past ten in the room at the end with the whiteboard nobody cleaned.

Nim said she thought the early clustering was worth a proper look.

I am going to set down what happened next in real time, because I have summarised it for a quarter of a century and the summary is a coward.

Rosalind said, 'Worth a look how?'

And Rosalind sat on the panel.

I have written that sentence in my head perhaps a hundred times over the years, always at night, and every time I have followed it with the observation that she would never have let it affect the appointment, which is true, because she was not that sort of woman.

It is true, and it is beside the point, and it took me about fifteen years to stop putting it in.

The post itself, since I have never described it and nobody has ever asked. I ran signal management for five years, which meant the number was mine. Not to set — the committee set it — but to tend: to know, week by week, what was rising toward it, what was sitting under it, what had learned, if a compound can be said to learn, to travel below the line. I was the person who watched the watching. There were nine of us by the end, and I was good to work for, I believe, in the narrow sense: I read everything, I credited people by name in writing, and I never once asked a question in a meeting to which I already knew the answer, having watched what that costs, and I am aware of what that sentence contains.

And through 2001 and into 2002 I checked. Not officially. There was no file open to check against. On the first Monday of the month, early, before the floor filled, I ran the Velmarid reporting rate myself, and it had not changed, exactly as I had worked out that it could not, and each month the not-changing confirmed two things at once — that I had been right, and that being right did not matter — which is the pair of findings this book keeps arriving at from different directions, and I logged nothing, and told nobody, and by the December of 2002 the listing had grown to three hundred and ninety rows, and one of the new rows, though I would not see it for another year, was a woman from Grimsby.

In the November of 2002 the reporting pattern still had not changed and I did not go back to it.

### Nine

Sheila Nunn.

I am going to do this without any of it. No arrangement. If it is badly written then it is badly written and it should be left exactly as it comes.

She was fifty-four. She lived in Grimsby. She died on the eighth of November 2002 and the certificate gives the cause as myocardial infarction, which was true.

I first saw the name in a line listing. That is a table. Each row is a person and the columns are age, sex, indication, dose, duration, outcome, reporter type. Her row was one hundred and sixty-something out of four hundred and eleven. I have never recovered the exact number and it has mattered to me more than it should.

She had been on it eleven weeks.

That is the column that has her in it. Eleven weeks is inside the window I spent nine days on in the June of 2000 and did not describe out loud on the eleventh of July.

I am being careful here, because this is the point at which a person in my position starts to build a chain, and the chain is not sound. I do not know that the drug killed her. Nobody knows that. She was fifty-four and she had established cardiovascular disease, and women of fifty-four with established cardiovascular disease die of myocardial infarction on the eighth of November every year, drug or no drug.

What I know is that she is in the period. Between my sixteen words and the four sentences in the bulletin there are forty-four months, and a number of people in those months died who would not otherwise have died, and that number is not knowable. I have seen it put at ninety and I have seen it put at six hundred, and I have read both papers, and I am professionally qualified to tell you that neither is reliable.

She is one of the rows. She is not a hypothesis. That is as far as it goes and as far as it has ever gone and I cannot make it smaller.

---

In the January of 2004 I looked her up.

There was an item in the Grimsby paper, seven lines, from the November. She had worked at the same firm for twenty-nine years, in the office, and they had done a collection. It gave her husband's name, which was Ray. It said she was a member of a choir.

That is everything that exists. I have looked many times since and there is nothing else.

I could recite the seven lines. I am not going to. They are hers. She was born before people were photographed constantly and she died before it either.

I telephoned the coroner's office in the February.

I had a note in front of me of what I intended to ask, which was a question about the recording of medication history in reports to the registrar, and which was a real question that I was entitled to ask in my post. I dialled it twice and put it down the first time.

The woman who answered was called Denise, which I have never been able to do anything with, since there is another Denise later in this book and they have nothing whatever to do with one another.

She was helpful and slightly bored. She went away and came back. She said the file would be in the annexe and did I want her to look, and I said if it was not any trouble, and she said it was no trouble at all and that it was better than what she had been doing.

She came back after a few minutes.

She said, 'Right. Nunn. Sheila Margaret Nunn.' And then, reading: 'Yes. There's nothing in here about tablets at all, love. It's just the certificate and the two forms.'

She said the name aloud twice.

She said it Grimsby. Sheela. Not Sheila as I had been saying it in my head for two months, but Sheela, flat, with the vowel pushed forward, the way my grandmother said certain words and the way I said them myself until I was about nineteen.

I said thank you very much and I put the phone down and I sat in the office with my hand still on it.

That is the thing I have that is not in any file. That is the whole of it. I know how her name sounded in the mouth of a woman who had never met her either.

Margaret I had not known.

---

I have thought, at various points over the years, about writing to Ray Nunn. I got as far as a draft twice, in 2006 and in 2011, and both drafts were good, and I am aware of what that means.

The 2006 draft is four paragraphs and opens with the weather in Grimsby that November, which I had looked up. The 2011 draft is shorter and worse, which is to say better, and neither contains the word sorry, because sorry makes it about the speaker, and I had learned that much at least from the letters I spent a career reading — the ones that came in with the cards sometimes, from the families, in biro, on lined paper, which we logged, and answered with a leaflet.

There is nothing I could have told him that would not have been about me. That is not modesty and it is not an excuse. It is that I have gone over it and over it, and every version of that letter ends up being a woman explaining that she has suffered too, and he would have had to write back, because people do, and he would have had to be kind to me.

So I did not write, and the not-writing is the only decision in this entire matter that I am certain was correct, and I should say that being certain about it has been very useful to me.

---

I am not going to say that I think about her every day, because I do not, and I have caught myself twice in the last month arranging a sentence that would suggest I do.

I think about her perhaps twenty times a year. It is not a schedule. It happens in the car, mostly, and once at a state dinner in a way I will not describe, because describing it would turn it into an anecdote.

What I feel is not guilt, or it is not the thing I understood guilt to be. Guilt appears to be a feeling that wants something. It wants to be discharged, or confessed, or converted into an improvement, and there is a whole industry that will help you do that, and I have watched people go through it and come out lighter.

This does not want anything. It is a fact about the world in the same way that the eighth of November is a fact, and it does not care whether I am aware of it, and it will be exactly the same size when I am dead, which will be soon.

---

I have had a small number of unbearable thoughts about all this and I am going to write down one of them.

If I had said it properly in that meeting, I would probably have been wrong. Not about the drug. About the evidence, at that date, on that table. Rosalind would have been within her rights, the committee would have wanted more, and it would have taken until the following spring to assemble, and by then Leuven were already recruiting, and the label would have changed at more or less the moment it actually did.

I have run this many times and I run it well, because running it is a thing I am good at, and on most of the runs it makes no difference at all.

And I understand perfectly that this changes nothing, because I did not stay quiet on the grounds that speaking would be ineffective. I stayed quiet because I was six days from a post I wanted and did not want a difficult summer. The other thing is arithmetic I have done afterwards, for a quarter of a century, at night, and it is very good arithmetic.

---

I have just had that read back to me and it is well made.

I asked for it to be read back because I wanted to be certain it was plain. It is not plain. It is worked. I did the working while I was speaking and I did not catch myself doing it, and I have now listened to four pages of a woman being moving about a dead stranger in exactly the rhythm she would use to be moving about anything.

I cannot get underneath it even here. Even for her. With the time I have.

I have always assumed that was a door I could open if I ever actually needed to.

This morning is the first occasion in my life on which I have properly tried, and I would like to stop for today.

I said of course, and thanked him, and for the next three months I answered the telephone the way you open a door with your foot behind it.

Martin noticed, incidentally. He said in the August, apropos of nothing, that I'd got very quick on the phone lately, and I said had I, and he said, 'Mm. Like your mother when the rates were due,' which was so exactly accurate about a woman he had met a dozen times that I laughed before I could arrange not to, and he was pleased, and went back to the boundary review, and I stood in the hall with my heart going, for the second time that summer, for the opposite reason.

She said, 'I'm told you were the person who actually understood the reporting side.'

She said, 'So a person in Croydon.'

I said, 'Effectively, yes.'

I bought it on the day, at the newsagent in the village, from a man called Mr Prentice who kept the intellectual weeklies in a short stack by the cat food, for me and, he once said, 'one other', and I have wondered for twenty years who the other was, and never asked, because a village is a system too, and I know better than most what happens when you start reading the margins.

My name is in it once, in a list of six officials who held relevant posts during the period, in the twenty-third paragraph, spelled correctly.

I bought two copies. I do not know why I bought two.

I left in the spring of 2005.

The reasons I have given, over the years, are these. That the merger in 2003 had changed the work and I did not enjoy the new shape of it, which is true, and which everybody in the building said. That my mother's affairs had taken longer to settle than I expected, which is true. I cleared the desk on the last afternoon into one box, which is the correct number of boxes for twenty-five years if you have been doing it properly all along, and most of the box was other people's kindnesses: a card, the print, a pebble Nim had brought back from somewhere and left on my monitor one morning without explanation, which I had never moved. ### Ten

The Prime Minister does not live at Number 10. Everybody who has been in the building knows this and it always disappoints people to be told, so I have generally not told them.

The flat above Number 11 is the larger one, four bedrooms, and it has been used by the Prime Minister's family since the Blairs swapped in 1997 because they had children and needed the room. It stuck. So the arrangement, by the time we arrived, was that the man running the country lived in the Chancellor's house and the Chancellor lived in his, and nobody thought this odd, and anybody who raised it was given a short history and a slightly pitying look.

Living there is like living in a very good flat over a very busy office. You come down the stairs and there is a country going on.

What follows is the longest chapter in this book. Eight years out of seventy-three: one chapter, however long the chapter has to be. That is the arithmetic, and it is also the argument.

The private corridor was not serviced after eight in the evening. Martin asked for that in the first month. It was written down somewhere and referred to, by everybody in the house, as the understanding. It is the only thing either of us ever asked for and it was, on reflection, an enormous thing to ask for, and it was granted inside four hours by people who were visibly relieved that this was all we wanted.

---

He had gone into Parliament at forty-nine, at a by-election, for reasons that had a good deal to do with a home in Sutton-in-Ashfield, and that part is his story, and he has declined to write it. The climb after that took twenty years, and I was there for all of it, and I am going to give it four sentences, because everybody else has given it chapters. He was liked. He was underestimated at every stage by exactly the people whose underestimation turned out to be useful. He asked his question. And at the end there was nobody left standing whom anybody preferred, and he got the job at sixty-nine, which the papers minded a good deal more than the country ever did.

And I ought to account for myself in those years, because I can see the gap from here, and if I do not fill it somebody else will.

From 2005 I did the casework. Not officially. Officially the constituency office had two paid staff and a rota of volunteers, and I was on the rota, on Wednesdays, as Dorrington, which had been my name and was, in that building, nobody's. What I actually did was the difficult third: the housing appeals, the benefits tribunals, the care assessments conducted in fifteen minutes by somebody with a laminated sheet. I had precedents. I had phrasing that survived contact with a decision-maker. I had, by the end, a success rate that I kept privately in a notebook and am not going to put here, because it would sound invented.

I did Christine's appeal out of love. These I did out of appetite, which is a different thing, and I could tell the difference every Wednesday at nine o'clock, and it felt like Thursday afternoons in the room over the boiler.

Nine hundred cases, more or less, in twelve years. Perhaps forty of the people I did them for ever learned my married name. It is the best work I have ever done, with the possible exception of one document signed by somebody else, and it is not in his books, and it is not going in mine, and so this is the only place it will ever be written down.

Now the policy, because I said I would, and because nobody else is going to.

He spent eleven years on adult social care. Not on the announcements about it, of which there were four and of which two were largely the same announcement. On the thing itself: the funding settlement, the workforce, the interface with health, the appalling and boring business of a hundred and fifty local authorities and their eleven different ways of counting the same person twice.

He got perhaps a third of it. The funding formula held. The workforce piece was abandoned in the second term, for reasons that were not his fault, that included the obvious one, and that I could set out and will not. Two of the better bits are being quietly reversed as I speak, by people who are not being malicious and who have a war on and a different problem in front of them.

A third is enormous. I have seen the arithmetic on what a third means in that sector, in beds and in hours and in women in their fifties not giving up work, and it is a considerable number of people having a slightly less terrible decade than they would otherwise have had.

It will not be in his obituaries. I have read two drafts of his obituary, which is a thing that happens to you, and it is not in either of them.

Chequers we had at weekends, when the diary allowed, which was less often than the diary pretended. It is a good house that nobody has ever loved, looked after by people who love it on the country's behalf, and it does one thing to every visitor: there is a window on the stair with Cromwell in it somewhere, or near it, and every guest of every rank pauses at that window and says something, and what they say is a small clean sample of exactly who they are, and I collected those sentences for eight years the way my grandmother followed the hedge. A foreign minister said, 'We have the same problem at home, only ours is still alive.' A researcher of about twenty-three said, 'Sorry — is this the actual stairs?' and was the only one I ever wanted to keep.

The boxes came even there. Two of them on a Saturday, one on a Sunday, carried up the drive in a car that had come from London for the purpose, and he did them at a desk by a window with the park going grey outside, and I read in the same room, and that, for eight years, was what a weekend was: two people at opposite ends of a borrowed room, each with their reading, meeting at meals, and I want it recorded that those were good evenings, some of the best, and that a marriage can be conducted at thirty feet as well as at three, and better, if the people in it are the people we were.

---

Denise Fry ran the household. She had been there through three of them before us and stayed for one after.

She was the single most competent person I have ever met, and I include everybody in this book, and I include Rosalind. She knew what was happening in that building at a level of granularity no official ever did. She never once told me anything and I never once asked, and we were extremely fond of one another for eight years within those limits.

She had a way of announcing weather. Not the sky's. The building's. 'Heavy day today, I should think,' she would say, setting something down, on mornings when nothing had been announced to anybody, and she was never once wrong, and I learned to read the flat off her the way the men downstairs read their screens, and I have wondered what she was reading it off in turn, and my best answer, after eight years of study, is shoes in the corridor and the volume of the telephones, and I am not satisfied with my answer.

The understanding was tested exactly once. In the second year, close to midnight, there was a knock at the corridor door — a box had gone up with the wrong day's papers in it, and a young man newer than young came to swap it, mortified, holding the replacement like a christening. Denise materialised behind him in a dressing gown of great authority, took the box out of his arms, said, 'I'll see it down, you see yourself,' and, to me, 'Nothing that can't be Tuesday's problem, madam,' and the corridor closed over the whole event the way the conversations used to close over the aircraft, and in eight years that is the entire military history of the understanding.

In about the third year I came down at ten past six in the morning and she was in the corridor with a cloth and a chair on its side, and there was a mark on the wall about a foot long at shoulder height, and she was taking it off.

I said, 'Goodness.'

She said, 'It's these chairs. They're too wide for this corridor and they always have been.' And then she said, 'Would you like tea taking up?'

And I said yes please, and went back up, and it was on the wall for another six hours before it wasn't, and I have never known what happened in that corridor and I did not ask then and I could have asked her at any point in the years that were left.

She sent a card when we left. It said *It was a pleasure* and then, underneath, *You never once made extra work. D.*

I have that. It is not in the roof. It is in the drawer of the table in this room.

---

I want to describe the dinners, because they are the thing people ask about, and because what people imagine is wrong in a specific way.

They imagine a table at which decisions are made. There is no such table. What there is, four or five evenings a week, is eight or ten people eating chicken in a room with the wrong acoustics, and every one of them has been awake since half past five, and the conversation is about ninety per cent process, eight per cent gossip and two per cent the actual thing.

The two per cent is real. It is not that the dinners do not matter. It is that somebody says something faintly indiscreet about a colleague's difficulty at about a quarter past nine, and everybody notes it, and four months later a job goes a particular way and nobody involved could tell you why.

Here is one, in the fifth year, because a specimen is worth more than a description.

Nine people. A Secretary of State and her husband, who taught. Two officials, one of whom I never saw again. Nadine. A man from the Bank who was there about something else and stayed. A backbencher who had been asked because it was easier to ask him than to keep not asking him. Martin. Me.

The chicken was very good. The chicken was always very good.

The backbencher told a long story about his selection in 1997 and got the year wrong by two, and nobody corrected him, and I watched four people decide separately not to.

The Secretary of State's husband asked me what I had done before, and I said medicines regulation, and he said, 'Oh, my brother-in-law's in pharma,' and then told me about his brother-in-law for nine minutes, and I asked him two questions about it because he was enjoying himself and because it kept me out of the other conversation.

At a quarter past nine the man from the Bank said, of somebody not present, 'He's very tired, isn't he,' and put a bit of bread in his mouth.

That was all. He did not elaborate and nobody asked him to and the conversation went somewhere else within about four seconds.

The man not present was moved in the January.

He was given a bank, I believe, or the branch of one, and he sent a Christmas card to the flat for years afterwards, addressed to us both, which I answered, because the wives answer, and every year the card was the same painting of geese, and I have wondered since whether he chose it once and forgot, or chose it fresh each year, and which of those is sadder.

The wives — and it was still mostly wives, with two husbands and a partner nobody had ruled on — had, unofficially, our own table, which convened perhaps three times a year at the residence of whichever embassy had the best pastry chef, and no minutes were taken, and everything was minuted, in nine heads, in six languages, and I have sat at peace tables with less information in the room. We did not trade secrets. We traded weather: whose husband was sleeping, whose principal had stopped listening to the building, who had begun to be photographed from the left. The table knew a resignation was coming, twice, before the resigning man did, and said nothing, in six languages, because saying was not what the table was for. Holding was what the table was for, and we held it.

---

At any of those dinners, the state ones and the chicken ones alike, I could tell you, by the pudding, which of them was frightened, which was drinking more than the week before, which two had had a row that afternoon, and which one had decided to go.

The craft of it, since I am unlikely to get another chance to lodge it anywhere: fear you saw at the bread. Frightened men buttered like surgeons, to the corners, taking their time, because it was the one operation in front of them that could not go wrong. A row you saw in the salt, passed with two hands or not passed at all. And the man who had decided to go was always, always, the most relaxed person at the table, better company than he had been in two years, generous about rivals, because the examination was over and he had marked his own paper and the marking was a mercy, and I have watched a version of that mercy arrive in my own mirror this year, at longer range, about a larger examination, and I recognised it at once from the dinners. I was right about that last one, I should think, four times out of five across the whole period.

I told Martin about four of them.

I told him lightly, in the way you pass on a remark, in the bedroom, at half past eleven, with the light off. He would say 'hm' and go to sleep. Some weeks later he would act on it, and would have no recollection whatever of where it had come from, and I would watch him do it and feel a thing I have never satisfactorily named and which is not pride and is not resentment and has some of each in it.

Once, in the seventh year, he said at breakfast, 'I had a thought about Graham,' and told me my own thought, back to me, in almost my own words, with real enthusiasm.

I said, 'That's interesting.'

---

The security people I never sorted out.

It looks like a failure of attention and it was not. There were, over eight years, fifteen or so men and a handful of women who came up those stairs with folders, from four different organisations, and I never established which was which.

Some of that was deliberate on their side. They do not introduce themselves the way other people do. Most of it was that I understood, early, that knowing would not be welcome, and that a wife who can distinguish between the agencies is a wife who is taking an interest, and I have never once taken a visible interest in anything.

So there is a man I called, privately, the one with the roses, because he grew them and talked about them at two Christmases and was extremely good on blackspot, and who was, I now believe, quite senior indeed, and whose surname I could not give you.

At the second of those Christmases, by the window, holding his glass at the angle of a man on his way elsewhere, he said, apropos the roses, 'Everything wants deadheading, Lady Bewley. The trick is deciding what you let flower first.' And then he was talking to the Chief Whip, and I stood with my drink and turned the sentence over, and I have never settled whether it was horticulture, and the not-settling was, I am fairly sure, the intended gift.

There was another one who always said 'ma'am' and one who always said 'Mrs Bewley' and one who, on four occasions across six years, said 'Alison' and each time corrected himself.

I looked him up after we left, the correcting one, in the way one now can, and found nothing, which was the correct finding, and closed the tab feeling watched, which was also, I assume, the correct finding.

---

The acronyms I never got either, and that one I have turned over a great deal more.

There were twenty or so in regular use in that flat. They arrived in conversation without expansion, from the beginning, and there is a window of about three weeks at the start of a thing like that in which you can ask what something stands for. After that you cannot, because you have been nodding.

I nodded for eight years.

I can still produce them. I could say six of them now, in the correct tone, in the correct part of a sentence, with the correct small pause before them, and any of those men would have understood me perfectly and would have assumed I knew what I was saying.

Some of them were ours. A good many were not.

That one I did work out, and it took me about two years, and nobody ever said it to me directly. There is a whole vocabulary in that building which does not come from this country. It arrives with systems that were built elsewhere and is used by people who were trained on them, and it has a flavour. You hear it in the way somebody says a word. You hear it in *program*.

And when it comes up in front of a wife at a dinner, there is a phrase, and the phrase is *our friends*.

I heard it three hundred times, I should think, in eight years. It is said with a particular flatness. It is never explained, and it does not need to be, because everybody at the table knows exactly which friends, and after a while so did I, and I could not tell you the day I learned it.

Once, and once only, a guest asked. A spouse, like me — the husband of somebody's counterpart, a retired engineer from a sensible country, who put down his fork in the middle of the flat vocabulary and said, pleasantly, 'Forgive me. What is that one? The one you all keep saying.' And the table did a thing I have never forgotten: it answered him. Fully, kindly, in about forty seconds, with an example. It had never been a secret. It had only ever been a door that nobody local could afford to be seen knocking at, and a man with a return flight walked through it for free, and I sat with my glass and did the arithmetic on what my own question would have cost in year one, and in year five, and that evening, and the numbers came out the way my numbers always come out, which is why I am telling you this at all.

For completeness: I did learn one of them, eventually, by accident, from a crossword of all places, on a train, and sat looking out of the window with the answer in my lap and the strange flat feeling of a door opening onto a cupboard, and I have never told a living soul which one it was, and the pleasure of that has lasted ever since and is not spent yet.

---

Guy Trescothick handled the press for the first four years. I liked him enormously and he is one of the three most frightening people I have ever met.

In the second year there was a photograph. A man had waited two days on the roof of the annexe and got Martin at the counter of the little kitchen upstairs, in a shirt, at about seven in the morning.

Guy came up about it. He had it printed on A4 and he put it on the table the way you put down a card.

He said, 'Right. Two things and neither of them is the one you're thinking.'

I said I was not thinking anything.

He said, 'You're thinking privacy. Nobody cares about privacy, it's the best picture of him anyone's ever taken and I'd have paid for it. The problem is over his shoulder.'

And he put his finger on the dishwasher.

He said, 'Because if that's too good, we've got a fortnight of it, and if it's not good enough it looks like we're saying something, and there's Alan's kitchen sitting there in everybody's memory from March.'

I said it was an ordinary machine.

He said, 'It looks ordinary to you. Everything looks ordinary to the person who owns it.'

They spent four working days on it. I know it was four because I asked at the end, out of what I told myself was amusement, and Guy said, 'Four.' And then, 'Four and a half, actually.' He was not embarrassed and he was right not to be, because that is the job.

They concluded that it was fine. It was an ordinary machine of an ordinary make. Everybody's mother had one.

That photograph is on the cover of two books about my husband. One of them is very hostile. Both of them use it to mean an ordinary man in an ordinary kitchen, and both of them are correct.

I have never once looked at it without noticing that the top basket is loaded wrong.

There was a second photograph on that roll, which Guy showed me once and the world has never seen: Martin at the same counter forty seconds later, laughing at something out of frame — at me, in fact, though nothing in the image says so — with the kettle mid-pour. It was, by any measure, the better photograph. Guy killed it without discussion. 'Happy reads as unserious,' he said, and put it back in the folder, and I have wanted that photograph ever since and never asked, because asking Guy for a print would have been an event, and I do not have events.

At Christmas the household had its party, below stairs, strictly theirs, and we were invited for the first half-hour only, which was correct, and Martin carved at Denise's instruction and wore the hat and was, for thirty minutes a year, simply a large man from Nottinghamshire being useful at a buffet, and I watched the staff watch him and understood that the building had taken a vote at some point, silently, the way buildings do, and that he had won it, and that no such vote had been held on me, or ever would be, because I had made sure there was nothing to vote on, in that building as in every other, and I stood by the trifle with my glass and was, in my fashion, content.

---

There is a version of that story which I have told at about forty dinners and which gets a very good laugh at the four and a half.

I told it again this morning. Josie has heard it twice now and laughed both times, which is professional of her, and she said afterwards that it was going to be one of the bits people quote.

I said I expected it would.

### Eleven

He had a habit and everybody in the building knew about it, and for about twenty years I found it faintly comic.

He would be given a thing. A submission, a recommendation, a paper with a decision at the end of it, worked on for weeks by people who were extremely good at their jobs. He would read it. There would be a pause. And then he would say: 'Wait. Go back.'

And then he would ask a question.

It was never a clever question. Everything about him is in that, so I had better get it right. It was almost always a stupid question, in the sense that a clever man would have been embarrassed to ask it. Where did this number come from. Who actually said that. Is this the same person as the one on page two. When you say we have consulted them, what does consulted mean here.

About seven times in ten the answer was fine and everybody moved on. About three times in ten there was a silence.

Nadine told me once that the private office had a name for the pause. I never found out what the name was and I did not ask, because asking would have been taking an interest.

---

I saw it happen properly twice, because wives are not in those rooms, and both times were at home.

The second one was in the sixth year, at Chequers, on a Sunday, in the room they call the Long Gallery which is not long. There were five of them and me, and I was there because it had started as lunch.

There was a paper about a procurement. I am not going to say which and it does not matter and it was not, in the end, important.

A woman whose name I never got — she was one of the ones who arrived by car and left by car and I saw her three times in eight years — took them through it for about twelve minutes. She was good. She had it entirely at her fingertips and she made a joke at minute nine that landed.

Martin had been holding the same page for a while.

He said, 'Wait. Go back.'

She said, 'Of course. Which part?'

He said, 'The nine authorities. It says here they've all been consulted.'

She said they had.

He said, 'Consulted how?'

There was a pause of about two seconds, and in the two seconds I watched a man on the sofa opposite stop looking at his phone.

She said that the department had written to all nine and had had responses from seven.

Martin said, 'And the two.'

She said the two had not responded within the window.

He said, 'Which two?'

She looked at the man with the phone. The man with the phone said he would have to check.

And Martin said — and this is the part I would put in a frame if I could — 'It'll be Cumbria and one other, and if it's Cumbria the whole thing doesn't work, because they're the only ones doing it the way this assumes. Can somebody find out before Tuesday.'

It was Cumbria. The thing was redone over about five weeks and came back different and I have no idea whether the different version was any good.

A fortnight after, an envelope came up with the household post, addressed to me, which nothing official ever was. A single card, no letterhead, two words: *Cumbria confirmed.* Unsigned, and it did not need signing, and I put it in a book I was reading and have never found it since, which she would find funny, whoever she was, wherever she is.

What I remember is that on the way out the woman said to him, entirely without resentment, 'How did you know it was Cumbria?'

And he said, 'I didn't. I've just been to Cumbria.'

That is the whole of it. That is exactly the level. A large plain man from Nottinghamshire who was not the cleverest person in any room he was ever in, asking at intervals whether the thing he had been handed was actually true, and having been, that month, to Cumbria.

I have spent a good deal of the last few years trying to decide whether that is a small thing or the largest thing there is, and I am not going to resolve it in this book.

---

He did it at home for a decade before anybody paid him for it. He did it about a plumber's estimate. He did it about Colin's account of a dispute over a fence, in 1996, and was right, and Colin has never forgiven him.

The fence, briefly, because it is the whole method in miniature. Colin told it at Christmas as a story about an unreasonable neighbour, and told it well, and had the room, and Martin, passing with plates, said, 'Wait. Go back. Whose ladder was it?' And it was the neighbour's ladder, and the whole story turned over like a card, and Colin said, 'Well — ' and the room laughed, and my brother's grievance, which he had been keeping like a pet, died in front of him at his own table, in four words about a ladder. He has told the story since, at other Christmases, with the ladder left out. Martin has never corrected it again. He says a man's entitled to his own fence. He does it now about the surveyor.

And once — once in forty-four years — he began it at me. The autumn I left the agency, over supper at the constituency house, I gave him the four reasons, fluently, in the order I gave them to everybody, and he put his fork down and said, 'Wait — ' and I looked at him, and he looked at me, and something in him did the arithmetic on where the question would have to go, and he picked the fork back up and said, ' — is there any more of the rice.' There was more of the rice. I have thought about that unfinished question more than about most of the finished ones in this book, and what I have concluded is that the habit knew, even if the man did not, and that between the two of them they decided to let me keep the account I had built, which is either the kindest thing that never happened to me or the failure underneath all the others, and on different mornings I have it differently.

When the second book about him said the habit was the defining feature of his premiership, he read the passage aloud to me and laughed and said, 'Well. That's the level, isn't it.'

---

Robert Kew was a special adviser for five of the eight years, and I did not much like him, and I was wrong.

He was clever in a way that announced itself, which I have never been able to forgive in anybody, and he had a habit of finishing other people's sentences correctly, which is worse than finishing them incorrectly.

He came to the flat thirty-odd times. He always ate. He would arrive having not eaten and would stand at the counter with a plate and talk with his mouth full, and I found that endearing for about the first year and then stopped.

What he ate mattered, I later thought. He ate whatever had been left out, cold, standing, at speed, like a man robbing his own house, and once, at about ten at night, he ate most of a loaf, in slices, with nothing on them, talking the whole time about a by-election, and I watched him do it and filed it under manners, when any woman with my training should have filed it under evidence.

At a party, a year or so after we left, a woman who assumed I already knew told me that during one particular period Robert Kew had been getting up at four every morning to sit with a child who was not sleeping, and that the child had not slept properly in the better part of a year, and that his marriage ended about a year after that, and that nobody in the building had been told any of it at the time.

Martin had said to me once, in the car, that Kew was carrying something.

I had said Kew was just pleased with himself.

He had said, 'Mm,' which is what he says when he is not going to argue and is not going to change his mind.

I am putting this in because it has stayed with me more than it deserves. Everybody in that building was operating on about five hours. Everybody had a thing at home. The whole apparatus of the state, at the level where it actually turns, is a floor of about forty people who are all slightly wrecked in ways that none of the others know about, and the decisions come out of that, and there is no other way for them to come out.

Nobody has ever put that in a book about government and I am not confident I have managed it here.

---

Now Marianne Speight, whom I have been putting off for three sessions.

She was in the Cabinet for four years. She was extremely good and she should have had the job after him and did not get it, for reasons that had nothing to do with her ability. We were not friends. We were perfectly civil for seven years and she came to the flat a dozen times.

At a dinner in the spring of the seventh year, eight of us at the long table, she said the thing.

I should set out who was there, because the arithmetic of the next four seconds depends on it. Marianne. A Minister of State called Douglas Kerrigan who had been at school with somebody and who was there for that reason. Nadine. Two officials. A woman from the party who ran something and whose name was Jill. Martin, at the far end, in a conversation with Kerrigan about a bypass. Me.

The talk had got round to whether the party had a problem with women, which it did, and various people had said the things one says, and Jill had said the thing about the pipeline that everybody said in those years.

And Marianne, who had had a glass and a half and who was not being cruel yet, turned to me and said: 'Alison could have done any of this. Any of it. She's the cleverest person at this table and she's spent thirty years handing round the potatoes.'

There was a laugh, because it was framed as a compliment. Nadine said something quick and kind about how I had run a national surveillance system, and it went round, and it would have ended there.

And then Marianne did not stop, which was the mistake.

She said, 'No, but I mean it. I've never understood it. I've genuinely never understood it, and I've watched it for seven years.' She had her glass in her hand and she was not slurring and she was not shouting. 'Because it isn't modesty, is it. You're not modest. You're the least modest person I've ever met, you're just quiet about it.'

Which was, I want to record, entirely accurate, and better put than I have ever put it, and I have had eight years.

And then she said: 'I think you're frightened of him.'

---

I want to be very exact about the next four seconds, because I have had a long time to look at them.

The first thing is that the table went cold. Not shocked. Cold, which is different and much more useful.

The second is that she was wrong. Flatly, factually wrong, in a way anybody who had spent four hours in a room with Martin and me would know was wrong. I have never been frightened of my husband for one second of forty-two years and there is not a person alive who could be made to believe otherwise. Martin, at the far end, had stopped talking about the bypass. I saw him stop.

And the third is that I knew, immediately, in the way you know a card is the wrong card, that she had just handed me the entire evening.

I put my hand flat on the cloth. I let it go about two seconds longer than was comfortable for anybody.

Then I said, warmly, with a little amusement in it:

'Oh, Marianne. He'd be so pleased.'

Six words. The table came apart with relief. Kerrigan laughed too loudly and said something about the bypass to nobody. Martin, from the far end, into the cold, said, 'What have I done now?' and everybody laughed again, and it was gone.

By the pudding, two people had made a point of being especially kind to me. Nadine spoke to her in the corridor afterwards, briefly, and I watched it through a doorway and could not hear it.

Marianne wrote the following week. It was a good letter. She said she had been unforgivable and that she had had a bad month and that none of that excused it.

I wrote back and said not at all, and that I had thought it was funny, and that she must come to us in the summer.

She never raised it again. She never raised anything with me again. She came twice more and was pleasant.

He has never asked me what Marianne said. Not in the car, not that year, not since. He had stopped talking about the bypass, I saw him stop, and the thing he chose to say was the one sentence in the English language that could take that table apart, and he said it in the voice of a man who has missed everything. I have never decided whether he heard. I notice that I have never decided.

---

She was right about the first part.

She got it three-quarters of the way and then put one wrong thing on the end, and the wrong thing was so obviously wrong that it took the true part down with it, and I used the wrong part to bury the true part, and I did it in six words in under four seconds with no preparation at all.

That is the most skilful thing I have ever done. I have thought about it once a fortnight, more or less, for two years.

And what I have never settled is whether I could have done it if she had stopped one sentence earlier.

If she had said *it isn't modesty, is it, you're just quiet about it* and then looked at me and waited, with eight people at the table and no wrong thing to take hold of, I do not know what I would have said.

I think about that considerably more than I think about the letter.

---

Nadine said to me in the corridor afterwards that I had been very gracious. She meant it kindly.

I have known Nadine Cullimore for eight years and she reads people for a living and she is extremely good at it, and in all that time I was never once able to tell whether she believed a word she said to me.

I have wondered since whether that was mutual and whether she would say the same thing about me, and whether the two of us stood in that corridor for eight years being the only person in the building who could see the other one, and never once said so, out of professional courtesy.

Marianne, for completeness, runs a review of something now and is very good on the radio, and when I am mentioned she is generous with a precision that tells me she remembers every word of that table, and we exchange cards, warm ones, and we are, in the manner of my whole life, closer for it never having been repaired.

Nadine sent flowers in April. The card said *Thinking of you both, N.*

### Twelve

The kitchen upstairs was not the kitchen. The kitchen was two floors down, had four people in it, and produced on an ordinary day between sixty and ninety meals.

Ours was at the end of the private corridor and had been a dressing room until somebody's refurbishment. It had a sink, a stove nobody used properly, a table with two chairs, a window on to the wall of the annexe, and a dishwasher.

Because of the understanding, we ate up there four or five nights a week off six plates that were ours and not the state's, and somebody had to deal with the plates, and for eight years the person who dealt with the plates was whichever of us was in the room when it stopped being possible to ignore them.

He did it. That first. He did it perhaps four times in ten, unprompted, without announcing it, and he did it badly.

---

The night before was a Monday and there was nothing wrong with it.

We had eaten at about nine because he had been at the House. Denise had left a thing under foil with a note on it, which she did on Mondays, and the note said *needs 20 mins, don't believe the oven*. It was fish pie. He had two helpings and said so twice.

He talked about a woman he had met that afternoon at something in Bolton who had been a magistrate for thirty-one years and who had told him a story about a boy she had seen four times. He told it well. He told it for about ten minutes.

The story, since it was the last of its kind: she had seen the boy four times across six years, shoplifting rising to affray, and the fourth time she recognised the coat — the same coat, outgrown — and adjourned, and rang somebody she was not strictly supposed to ring. And the boy, which was the point of the story, the reason it got its ten minutes, was the man who had organised the hall that afternoon, and who had introduced her to Martin, thirty years on, as 'the one who noticed the coat'. Martin did the introduction in both voices. He was so pleased with it. Somebody had noticed something in time, once, in Bolton, and the system had a happy ending to show a visitor, and he brought it home to me like a find, and we went to bed on it.

I have thought since — not that night, I claim nothing for that night — that the folder waiting in the study held the other ending of the same story: a boy seen four times, and no fourth time adjourned, and the coat outgrown years before anybody official was looking, and I make no more of the pairing than this, that the last story my husband told me before the fourteenth of May was about somebody noticing in time, and he told it for ten minutes, and he was so pleased, and I have never once been able to hear it since as anything but a man showing his wife the thing he most needed to believe the job could be.

I told him about Christine, who had rung, and about Alec, who had had a bad fortnight, and he said the right things and he meant them, and he asked whether Christine was managing, and I said no, and he said, 'No.'

Then he stacked the plates by the sink, which is as far as he ever got at night, and went to bed at about twenty past eleven and I stayed up for half an hour with a book I have never been able to remember the title of.

I want it understood that there was nothing in that evening. I have been over it. There is a version of this morning in which it is the culmination of something and it was not the culmination of anything. It was a Monday and there was fish pie.

---

It was the fourteenth. I said the thirteenth for a long time and I was corrected in this room in April by a man who was right about it, for reasons involving a lunch.

He had been up since four. I know that because the study door was open when I came down the corridor, which it was not on the mornings when he had slept, and because there were two cups on the table and one of them was cold and had been used.

I came in at about a quarter past six. I had slept, and slept well, and that should be recorded, because a good deal has been written since about how tired everybody was that spring, and I was not tired.

The machine had been run in the small hours and not emptied. The radio had been moved along the counter since the night before, which meant he had already been in and tried it. It never worked in that room, and he attempted it every morning for eight years with total optimism.

The radio was a small black thing left behind by somebody's predecessor and kept out of respect for the leaving, and the fault was the annexe wall, which ate the signal, and everybody knew the fault was the wall, including him, and he tried it every morning anyway, moving it along the counter in quarter-inches, like a man humouring a cat, because eight years is too long to live alongside a thing and not offer it its chance daily. If you want the difference between my husband and me in one appliance, there it is. I would have thrown it out in the first week. He gave it two thousand mornings. Neither of us was wrong, and only one of us was loved by a building.

I opened it. There was the smell they have, hot plastic and lemon. The top basket was as he had left it, which is to say the two long knives were standing up in the little basket, and there were three cups on the bottom shelf where cups do not go, and the blue one was on its side.

The blue cup was Doreen's. Last of a set of six, the other five broken across four decades in the ordinary ways, and it had survived three kitchens and a government, and he drank from it every morning of his premiership without once, I think, knowing why his hand went to it, and I knew, and never said, because there are things you do not hand a man while he is holding a country.

---

Aled Prosser had come up the private corridor at twenty past six.

That happened in the middle of all of the above, and I have put it after, and I am aware that I have put it after.

He was twenty-nine or thirty. He had been in the private office about fourteen months. He was from Carmarthen and he had a very good suit that did not fit him and he had, at some point in the previous year, worked out that I was the person in that flat who noticed whether people had eaten.

He had a folder.

He apologised for the hour twice. He had clearly been told to apologise for the hour and had also independently decided to, which is the whole of him. He stood in the doorway of the little kitchen and did not come in, because the understanding covered the corridor and he had been briefed on it in his first week and had never once tested it.

He had been up all night too. That is what I have gone back to, and I have gone back to it perhaps five hundred times, and there is nothing in it, and I keep going back. Here is the second version, which I gave three weeks later without intending to and which differs in no fact.

It differs in that in the second version I know what I said about the second basket.

I have three candidate sentences. They are all reasonable and I have no way of choosing between them, and I would say to anybody who is confident about the wording of something they said in a kitchen twenty years ago, or in my case two, that they are describing a thing they have made and not a thing they have kept.

I will set the three down, once, and never again, and you may notice what they have in common, because I have.

*Shall I show you the second basket again?*

*It goes in facing out, my love. We have done this.*

*Leave it — it's quicker if it's done properly.*

That is what makes them candidates, and what makes choosing between them worthless: whichever one it was, it was the register that carried it, on that morning, at that hour, to a man holding what he was holding — and the register did not need the words any more than the machine downstairs needed to know whose name was on the folder.

Which was not anger. I have been asked what it was and I have said, four times now, that it was not anger, and I notice that I have never once said what it was.

### Thirteen

Josie asks, this morning, what he was like at breakfast.

She has a list somewhere, not on paper, and the breakfast question has been on it since the first Friday, and she has waited until the morning after we finished the kitchen material to put it down in front of me, which is the correct place for it, and I would have done the same.

We spent four days on the six minutes. I have said that already, in the chapter you have just read, which is not in the book.

He was in the car at eight minutes past seven, and by every account of that day, including the inquiry's, he was entirely himself. I know what the chapter is for. It is the only thing in either book that I know completely. And the sentence that says it cannot be said to Josie, or to the reader of the book, or, on the evidence of four days, to the machine. I was not overruled. Write that down, whoever you are. I agreed on the Thursday, and I have not raised it since, and she was right on every count she made, and the count she could not make is the reason there are two of everything.

### Fourteen

The statement to the House lasted a little under seven minutes and contained the word regret once and the word lawful five times.

I watched it from the flat, on the small television in the room that was called the study and was not a study, with Denise, who had come up with tea nobody had asked for, which was her way of being somewhere without being anywhere, and the two of us stood, for some reason, neither of us sat, and he said the sentences that had been built for him that morning by four people, and he said them well, and I could hear which clauses he had changed, because the changed ones scanned.

He did not name the town. He did not name the man. He named the legal basis, and the fact of prior advice, and the phrase that was in every paper by six o'clock, which was *a specific, imminent and continuing threat to life within the United Kingdom*, and then he sat down, and the House did the thing the House does when it has decided to be grave, which is a sort of collective lowering, like water finding a level.

Denise took the tray down when it was over and paused at the door and said, 'That's that, then, madam,' which was the household's entire editorial on the matter, delivered on behalf of the building, and more use to me than the four columns of analysis I read the next morning, all of which were by people who knew less and were surer.

He came up afterwards for ten minutes before the calls resumed, and drank most of a cup of tea standing at the window with his back to the room. Denise's tray had gone down barely touched for three days, which was her ledger of the week and more accurate than anything published. I asked whether he had eaten and he said he thought so. Then he said, not turning round, 'The lawyers are pleased with it,' in a voice I had not heard him use before and did not hear again, and I have spent two years failing to name what was in it, and the nearest I have got is: a man reporting that his coat had come back from the cleaners, on the day of a funeral.

That was the sixteenth of May, in the afternoon. The signature had been at ten past eight, two days before. The strike had been the same night.

The next morning's papers I read in full, at the little table, in the quiet after he had gone down, and the strangest part of the whole strange season was how quickly the event acquired furniture. By Saturday there were diagrams. By Sunday there were profiles of men with titles I had nodded at over dinner, rendered as strategists, with photographs eight years old. By the Wednesday there was a colour piece about the town, filed from a neighbouring country, describing a river the writer had plainly never stood beside, and I read it twice, professionally, the way I used to read a badly coded card, sorting what was seen from what was supplied, and there was one detail in it that had the grain of the actually seen — a bus shelter, repainted municipal green, half done, the job abandoned mid-panel — and I have never been able to leave that bus shelter, because somebody began painting it before, and did not finish it after, and that is the entire war, in one item of street furniture, in a town whose name I could spell for you and will not.

---

Here is the shape of it, as flat as I can make it, from the public record and the inquiry and the one thing I was told in a kitchen.

There was a man from this county. He was recruited at twenty-three through an account on a telephone application, by people he never met, and he went out through Poland the year the world reopened, and by the end he was not a fighter of any kind. He was a coordinator. He ran money and instructions to people in this country who set fires for it: a warehouse in the spring, full of generators and dressings and equipment for elsewhere; a lorry yard in the autumn; two young men from Erith who are in prison now under an Act that did not exist when I left the agency, and who were paid, between them, rather less than we spent on the funeral flowers of a colleague that year.

The next fire, the assessment said, was not going to be a warehouse.

He was in a town in the west of Russia, of about nine thousand people, whose name I could find on a map now in the dark. He had been there ten months. He was watched for most of them.

The town has a river and a bus garage and a teacher-training college that closed in the nineties, and the street he lived on is named for a poet, and none of that is in the inquiry, and all of it is in the folder, because Ffion Meares is not the only person who ever learned that officials engage differently with places than with grid references, and the official I was working on was me.

The aircraft was not ours. The missile was not ours. The launch was not ours, and the government of the country that launched it has never confirmed that it happened, and neither have we, and the government of the country it happened in announced it within four hours, loudly, with photographs, and was disbelieved by default, because it announces many things.

What was ours was everything that made it accurate. The list. The footage. The voice files. The pattern of his months. The map with the timings on it. And the page at the bottom of the folder that required a signature, and the signature it required was my husband's, and the reason it required his and nobody else's is that the man held a British passport, and for that, the law wants a name and not a committee.

There is a box on that page. I have never seen the page and I do not need to; I have spent my life in the system of boxes and I know the family resemblance at any distance. A doctor in Carlisle has a box, and a coder in Croydon has a box, and a committee has a line, and at the top of the whole ascending apparatus, on the worst mornings, there is one more box, and my husband's name goes in it in his own hand, and the margins of that page are empty, and they are the only empty margins in this entire book, because at that altitude nobody writes *she thinks it's the tablets*, and nobody writes *is it me?*, and nobody is allowed to write *mothers usually right*.

Our friends supplied a great deal. I will not be more exact than that, because I cannot be, because nobody in that flat was ever more exact than that in front of me, in eight years.

---

He came down to the kitchen at twenty-five to seven and I said something about cups.

You have had that. It is not in this chapter because this chapter is the one Josie says writes itself, and she is right, and I am watching it do so.

---

The people who died alongside him were not counted by anyone with the standing to count them.

That is not an accusation. I have spent my life inside the question of who counts what, and I am telling you the mechanism, not the crime. There was no access. There was no forensic basis. The figures that exist come from a belligerent, and are therefore treated as propaganda, and are partly propaganda, and the number of people who died in that street runs, depending on your source, from four to twenty-five.

A range from a number to more than six times the number. I knew the shape. I had been carrying its twin for twenty years, and when I saw the first estimates side by side in the paper I put the paper down and went and stood in the garden for a while, and Martin asked through the window whether I wanted anything, and I said no, and that was true.

The inquiry did not count them either, because it was not asked to. The inquiry was asked whether the process had been followed, and it found that the process had been followed, and it reported on a Thursday in July, and the report is four hundred pages, and the operative sentence is one. The committee that oversees these things reported separately that it had not been given everything it asked for, in language so polished by use that it slid off the day it was published. I read all four hundred pages in two sittings, professionally, the way I read everything, and the operative sentence, for the record kept in this drawer, is this one: *We have not seen the material on which the assessment of imminence was based.* Fourteen words, in the first person plural, on page three hundred and something, and every word of it is load-bearing, and it was quoted nowhere, because it does not scan, and the sentences that were built to scan were quoted everywhere. I know how that works. I have written both kinds. A member of the other House and a barrister I once sat next to at dinner brought a challenge, and the challenge failed, in the autumn, on standing, which means the court never reached the question, which is a sentence I have now written about two different courts in two different decades.

The barrister, at that dinner, years before any of it, had told me that the constitution of this country was a gentleman's agreement among people who were no longer gentlemen, and I had laughed and filed it under wit. In the autumn I watched her on the steps of the court saying measured things about standing, in the rain, to four cameras, and understood that she had been filing too, all along, and that hers was the better system, because she had filed it under warnings.

Nobody was punished. Nobody was promoted, either, which the older cases had led me to expect; the machine has learned that much. The government has never confirmed the operation, and will never confirm it, and my husband, who signed it, is not permitted to discuss it, and so he is the only man in England who has been cheered for a thing that officially did not occur. There is a photograph of him leaving the Palace the following week that did the work a statue does. It is a very good photograph. He looks tired and certain, and he was one of those things.

The word that season was resolve. He had shown resolve; the country was praised for its resolve; resolve was measured, somehow, and found adequate. Nobody used the other words, the ones with people in them, and I watched the vocabulary do its work the way I had once watched coding dictionaries do theirs: the event entered the language in the form in which it could be filed.

---

For three weeks the country braced.

Nobody used the word braced. The papers used the word response, in headlines with question marks — WHAT PRICE?, THE WAITING GAME, one simply WHEN — and the airports were quietly fuller of the kind of men who stand still well, and there was a Cobra meeting that was announced and two that were not, and Denise, who never told me anything, mentioned that the household's fire drill had been moved up, in the way you would mention a delivery.

The supermarkets had a fortnight of quiet, deliberate fullness, which the trade press noticed and the news did not: bottled water, batteries, the flat tins. Pat rang to ask, in a roundabout way that took ten minutes, whether Martin thought — and then did not finish the question, and I said we weren't worried, which was the household line and also true, in the way that a handrail is true. Hannah sent a photograph of her building's new evacuation notice with the caption *cheery!!*, and one of the men who said ma'am was in the flat more that month than in the year before, and stood at windows, which they are trained out of, so I understood he was senior enough to have opinions about his own training.

And once, in the second week — we had gone to the constituency house for the weekend, because the diary said normality and meant it as an instruction — a pair of ours went over, low, going east, and I stood in that garden, sixty years of training, waiting, and they were gone, and I was holding the washing to my chest, and I could not tell you when I had picked it up.

I found that I was listening again. I told you, at the beginning of this book, about the aircraft at Waddington, and how you learn to wait, and how a conversation opens a gap in itself and closes over. For three weeks in the spring of that year the whole country was a street in Bracebridge Heath, waiting for a noise it had learned, and I was seventy-one years old and back on the cliff with my grandmother, who never once mentioned them.

In the first week there was footage, twice, of a woman at a door in Sleaford saying, 'Not today, thank you,' to a street of cameras, in the tone you would use for a catalogue seller, and closing the door, not hard, with the click held in. I did not know then who she was to me. I knew the door-closing. I had grown up inside doors closed exactly like that, and I said to the empty room, the second time they ran it, 'Good for you,' out loud, and did not examine the remark, and it has since been examined.

Christine rang every Thursday of the three weeks, as she had in the nineties, without announcement, at the same hour, and talked about nothing, and it held, the way it held then, and neither of us has ever named the practice, which is a form of the practice.

Nothing came.

I want to be accurate about what the nothing was like, because it is the part nobody has written about, and it is the part I would keep if I could keep one thing from that year. The nothing arrived gradually, the way the noise had left Cliff Rise. There was no morning on which it was over. There was a week in which the question marks left the headlines, and a later week in which the men at the airports thinned, and a morning, at some point, on which I realised I had stopped listening again, and could not tell you when I had stopped, and that is twice in one lifetime, which seems excessive.

The not-coming was worse than an answer. An answer is an event, and events end. What we got instead was a demonstration, conducted on the whole population at once, that it had not mattered. A man from Sleaford was dead, and between four and twenty-five people around him, and the world had rebalanced itself with the small sideways settle of a lorry taking a load, and everyone went back to the price of things.

That is the vindication. Not the inquiry, not the photograph. The lorry settling. Nobody says it and everybody carries it: we did that, and it cost nothing, and things that cost nothing get done again.

---

I have a folder of my own on him. I may as well say it here, because it is in the drawer this book lives in, and whoever you are, you will find it under these pages. I keep the stack in the order things arrived, and I will tell you the order at the end, because by then it will be finished, and because I know what I have taught you to do with my counting.

I have read everything. That is not a figure of speech; the public record on this man is finite and I have finished it. I know the name of his art teacher, who is called Mrs Enderby and who told a newspaper one true thing and then stopped giving interviews. The true thing was this: 'He wasn't led astray. Astray was the only place anybody ever offered to lead him.' She was retired by the time she said it, and the paper ran it under a photograph of the school gates, and within a week she had been made to mean six different things by six different columnists, and she went quiet, having discovered what happens to a true sentence out of doors, which is what happens to a margin note on a yellow card: it cannot be counted, so it is used. I know he did a mural in year nine, of a lifeboat, and that it was painted over during a refurbishment before anybody knew to keep it. I know the builders' merchant on the Boston road, and the name of the manager who described him, accurately I think, as no trouble, which is a sentence I have heard applied to myself. I know what his sister said in 2019, before any of it, in a local paper, about something else entirely, and it is the only thing anybody in that family ever said in public while it could still have made a difference, and nobody read it, including me.

None of it accounts for him. I want that written down by somebody who is professionally qualified in the finding of signals: there is no signal. There is a boy of no trouble, and a mural of a lifeboat, and a telephone, and then there is a coordinator of fires, and between them there is a gap that the reporting does not cross and the inquiry does not cross and I cannot cross, and I have two folders in this book already that say the same thing in their own languages. No cause identified. Not inconsistent with the underlying condition. This is the third, and it is the fattest, and it is the same folder.

---

I have not written his name.

I noticed it a week ago, going back through these pages. Sheila Nunn is named in this book. Full name, spelled, pronounced, taken down over a telephone and carried for twenty years. And the man my husband killed has been *the man* for fourteen chapters, and I did not decide that. I sat down this morning intending to put his name at the top of this chapter, the way you put a name on a file, and my hand declined.

I have been saving it. I did not know I was saving it, and I do not yet know what for, and I am not going to force it, and I notice I have kept the verb for something.

Next week we do the room.

### Fifteen

The room was on the first floor of a building in Grantham that the council lets out for training days, and it had a hot-water urn on a trolley by the wall, and a window with vertical blinds, and carpet tiles in two blues, and I have been in several hundred rooms like it, on the other side of the table, doing the casework, and I had never once until that morning understood what the room looks like from the family's chairs.

It was nine weeks after the strike. The visit was Nadine's design, mostly.

There was a cord for the blinds that had lost its weight, and it ticked against the glass in the draught the whole time, and I counted it, God help me, out of habit, under everything, and it stood at ninety-one when Lorna said her son's name the fourth time, and I have that number still, and no use for it, and it may be the truest thing in this chapter. There is a document about it somewhere, which I have seen, because everything I do generates a document, and the document is two pages, and it uses the word *handling* four times and the word *grief* once, in a quotation.

It also contains, in an annex, a suggested form of words for me, drafted by somebody young and kind, for use *if directly addressed*. I have kept my copy, because it is the only script anybody wrote me in eight years, everyone else having correctly assumed I did not need one, and because the line itself — *we think of your family every day* — is a decent sentence ruined by *we*, and I spent the drive up, on and off, changing the pronoun in my head, and could not make *I* true either, and that is the whole of my life in a car on the A1: a woman revising a condolence she was not expecting to be asked for, so as not to be caught out by a grief that was not hers, in words that were nobody's.

The advice was against. I want that recorded, because it is to his credit and nobody knows it. The advice said no meeting, on legal grounds and on press grounds and on the general ground that nothing said in that room could help and several things could harm. Martin read the advice and said, 'Yes. And we're going,' and that was one of perhaps five times in eight years that I watched him spend the job on something, and the other four are in the book.

I asked to come. Nobody asked me. I asked, in the kitchen, the evening the visit was settled, and he looked at me for a moment and said, 'Yes. Good,' and did not ask why, and I could not have told him why, and I cannot fully tell you.

We drove up the A1 in the July heat with the country doing its summer along both sides, and Martin read papers for the first hour and then stopped, and we did not talk, and somewhere past Peterborough he took my hand on the seat and held it, not looking up, the way you hold a rail. The protection men in front had turned the radio off at Hatfield without being asked. There is a service station at the county edge that I have known all my life, and we went past it at speed, lights on in the daytime, and I thought, with no warning at all, of chips in a lay-by, and of everything a county is, and by the time I had the thought in order we were in Lincolnshire, where I am always in order, and the room was forty minutes away.

---

Lorna Hollick was sixty-two that summer, and she had run the office of a secondary school for thirty-one years, which I did not need the briefing to tell me, because I would have known it from the way she had arranged the chairs.

She had moved them. The building put us in by the back stairs, past a corridor of framed photographs of mayors, and a council officer whose name I did not keep walked ahead of us the whole way saying 'just along here' at intervals, to the corridor generally, in the voice of a man who had rehearsed a longer speech and thought better of it. The room had been aired. Somebody had aired it that morning, on purpose, for us, and I noticed, because noticing what has been done for you invisibly is the whole of my training.

It was her council, her town, her room in some sense, and she had turned the two chairs on their side of the table very slightly toward the window, so that the light fell across rather than into the faces, which is a thing you do if you have spent three decades watching frightened parents sit down opposite authority, and it undid me a little, before anybody had said anything, that she had thought of our comfort at all.

The urn came to the boil twice during the four minutes and clicked itself off, twice, the only other thing in the room with a job.

She stood up when we came in. Her daughter did not.

And she said, 'Thank you for coming,' in the vowels.

I have written in this book about the sanding. Forty years of it, the long a's flattened out of me at Nottingham and in London until my own mother said, in 1979, not unkindly, that I sounded like the radio. Lorna Hollick said four words and I was nine years old on Cliff Rise, and it took everything I have, and I have a great deal, not to answer her in kind, because it would have been the truest thing I did that year and it would have looked like technique.

She said her son's name in the second sentence.

She said it four times in the four minutes. Ryan. Not *my son*, not *he*, which is what the documents say, and not once with any special weight, because it was not a special word to her, it was his name, she had been saying it for twenty-seven years, across playgrounds and up staircases, and she was not going to stop using it because the rest of the country had started.

I had not said it once in nine weeks. I have not written it in fourteen chapters. I am taking it from her now, the way I took Sheila's from a bored woman in a coroner's office: secondhand, pronounced properly, too late.

Ryan. Ryan Hollick.

There. And whatever the last chapters of this book cost me, that is the sentence that cost the most, and I notice it looks like nothing.

---

What she said, in the middle of the four minutes, with her office hands laid flat on the table — kept nice, a wedding ring worn thin, laid down the way you steady paper — was this. I am reproducing it exactly. It is the one passage that is identical in the book and in the drawer, word for word, because I would sooner have cut my own chapters than sanded hers.

'I don't blame your husband. I want you to hear me say it, because you'll be told I do. I blame the ones who got to Ryan when he was twenty-three and nobody noticed. Not you, not the school, not me. Nobody noticed. Your husband came in at the end of a very long queue, and I haven't the anger to walk all the way back down it, so I've put it down. I want him to know the fires weren't Ryan. The money was Ryan, and the fires came out of the money, and I know that's not a difference in law. It's a difference to me.'

Martin said something then that was right, and short, and not in any briefing, and I am not going to reproduce it, because it is his, and it is the only thing from that room he has ever declined to have quoted, and I asked him once and he said, 'No. That was for her.' He is a better man than this book has room to show. I keep writing that sentence and cutting it. It stays this time.

That evening, home, he sat at the kitchen table for a long time after the plates were gone, with his hands flat on the wood the way hers had been flat on the laminate, though he could not have known he was doing it, and I did not tell him, and at about ten he said, 'She'd arranged the chairs,' and nothing else, and went up, and I sat on in the kitchen with the one lamp, and that is the closest the two of us have ever come to discussing the whole of it, and it was close enough, and it was eight words.

---

Kelly Hollick sat through all of it with her coat on.

The coat was good. Interview-good, bought that year for the cameras, and she wore it indoors like armour that had been paid for at cost, which it had.

She was thirty-four, and she is a dental nurse, and nine days before the meeting she had said, on a breakfast programme, with her mother sitting rigid beside her, the sentence that everybody of a certain cast of mind spent that summer pretending was outrageous:

'The only time this country ever decided Ryan belonged to it was when belonging to it meant they were allowed to kill him.'

That sentence is true. I am professionally qualified in the reading of documents, and I have read the legal advice, and the whole architecture of the thing runs through the passport, and Kelly Hollick, who left school at sixteen, put the entire jurisprudence in twenty-three words on a breakfast sofa, and was monstered for it for a fortnight, and was right.

In the room she said three things. She said, 'Is this being minuted,' at the beginning, to Nadine, who said no, which was true in the narrow sense. She said nothing at all in the middle, while her mother spoke, and she looked at me while her mother spoke, not at Martin, at me, the whole time, and I have thought about that look more than I have thought about anything else in that room. And at the end, when the ending had been reached in the way endings are reached, by everyone's weight shifting at once, she said, 'Four minutes,' to nobody, at the ceiling.

She had timed us.

Here is what I did, and here is where the two books part company, so I will do the book first.

In the book, I said the correct thing.

The book's account of that room runs to a dozen paragraphs, and it is accurate about Lorna to the syllable, and it says of Kelly only that 'her daughter, who had been badly treated by the coverage, chose silence, and it was a silence with dignity in it.' And it says of me: 'I stayed until they were ready for us to go. There was nothing to say, and I did not pretend otherwise.'

Reviewers will call that passage unsparing. I have been told by three separate people at the publisher that it is the finest thing in the book.

I made those pages for the book on a Thursday, in this room, in the ordinary way, and I want the manufacture on the record, because it is the one crime in this book that I can show you being committed. I heard the sentence coming. *A silence with dignity in it.* I heard it form, and I heard it get its third draft before it left my mouth, in the room voice, the good voice, and Josie's black pen took it down, and the machine took it down, and no alarm sounded anywhere in the world, because there is no alarm; that is the finding of my whole professional life arriving home at last. The system records what is said in the correct format. The margins are where the truth goes, and the margins cannot be counted, and I am the margin now, and you are reading it. It contains one invented silence, one invented dignity, and one sentence about myself with two lies in ten words, and nobody will ever check any of it, because the only people who could are a woman who will not read her own grief retailed and a woman nobody believes.

And Nadine. Nadine was in the room, Nadine reads everything, and Nadine is the one person in public life professionally incapable of mentioning what she knows. I thought about her for a whole evening before the extract ran, and then I stopped, because I have still never been able to tell whether Nadine believes a word I say, and that has finally become useful.

Here is the drawer's account.

Kelly's silence had no dignity in it. It was a held breath. It was the silence of a woman who has worked out that anything she says will be used to make her mother's composure look like the family's real position, and who is spending every ounce she has on not handing us that, and it cost her more than her mother's forgiveness cost her mother, and I watched her pay it out, second by second, with her coat on, and what I felt was not compassion.

I felt contempt. There it is. She was graceless, and she had been graceless on television, and she was the only person in that room being fully accurate, and I, who have spent seventy years being graceful and inaccurate in rooms exactly like it, sat opposite a woman doing the opposite and I despised her for it, quietly, behind a face that three decades of dinners built, and I did not even register it as an event at the time. That is the part I would have you sit with. Not that I felt it. That it did not register. Contempt had become, by then, a background process, like the machine that codes the cards.

And 'I stayed until they were ready for us to go' is false. We left after four minutes because I touched Martin's arm. Not Nadine's signal. Mine. I could not be in the room with Kelly Hollick's held breath for a fifth minute, and I moved us out, with one touch, using twenty-six years of knowing exactly what my hand on his sleeve does in company, and Lorna stood, and thanked us for coming, in the vowels, again.

In the car, on the A52, I said to Nadine, 'The daughter is enjoying this.'

Nadine said, 'Mm.'

Nine days later I watched the breakfast programme again, the clip of it, alone, in the flat, twice, and I have known from that morning to this one exactly what I said in that car, and I have now written it down, and I am not going to arrange anything around it. That is the worst sentence in this book. There are sentences in this book about the deaths of strangers and the death of my mother, and the worst one is five words in a car on the A52, and I knew it was the worst one before the car reached Grantham station, and I have carried it the way I carry things, which is completely, and at no visible cost, and here it is.

The correction, when it came, two years later, through the publisher, was about Ryan's age.

But that belongs to the spring, and I find I want one ordinary thing before it. There is not much time left in the mornings. The hand is what the hand is. I have started underlining the words I am sure of.

### Sixteen

Hannah asked me in March, in the kitchen, with her coat half off, whether I ever minded.

I have owed you this since the second chapter, and I have been carrying it round the book looking for a place to put it down, and there is no good place, so it goes here, between the room and the rest, which is where it happened.

She had driven over from Nottingham on a Saturday with bulbs for the pots, because she had decided the pots needed bulbs, which is her way of visiting a dying woman without either of us having to call it that. She was planting them at the sink end while I sat, and she was talking about the book, which she is excited about in a way nobody else is, and she said, not carefully, not leading up to it, with soil on her hands:

'Did you ever mind, though? Honestly. All of it. Being the one who — you know. Did you ever mind?'

There it was. Sixty years. And the rest of the visit, for the record, because the rest is the proof: she finished the pots, and washed her hands at my sink talking about Colin's knee, and made the tea wrong in the way she makes it, which I drink because she made it, and kissed the top of my head going, which nobody else alive is licensed to do, and beeped the horn twice at the end of the drive, which is her signature, and was gone by half past four, entirely unburdened, which had been the whole object of the exercise, achieved with one sentence, and cheap at the price. Christine came within a sentence of it in a kitchen in 1979 and was deflected without effort. Marianne got three-quarters of the way at a dinner and handed me the means of her own dismissal. Frank looked at his desk. Rosalind put down a pencil. And a physiotherapist with soil on her hands, thirty-four years old, who has me entirely wrong, who believes there was a career I laid down for Martin like a coat over a puddle, asked the question flat out, on a Saturday, with no agenda except that she loves me, and the door I have been describing for fifteen chapters stood all the way open in my own kitchen.

I said: 'You make a life out of what's in front of you, love. And I was lucky in what was in front of me.'

Both of those sentences are true. Together they are the cheapest lie in this book. She stopped planting, and came over, and took my hand with the soil still on hers, and believed me — believed me with her whole face, the way that family believes things — and that is what the lie was for, and it worked, and I have written it out now and I find it is the only lie in this book I would tell again tomorrow.

Because here is the thing I understood while she was holding my hand. The true answer was available. I could feel it in my mouth. And the true answer, delivered to Hannah, does not set Hannah free. It hands a thirty-four-year-old woman who admires me a lifetime's instruction that the admired life was a withholding, three months before I die, and then leaves her alone with it. The lie was not for me. For once. I have checked this from every side the way I check everything, and I keep arriving at the same place, and the place is: the lie was the kind thing, and I am not to be trusted on that conclusion, and both of those are true, and that is where I am leaving it.

She is thirty-four. Kelly Hollick is thirty-four. The woman who read me truly got my contempt, and the woman who has me entirely wrong got my heart, and they are the same age, and I noticed it in the room in Grantham, and I have declined to do anything with it until this sentence, and I am not going to do anything with it now either, except put it where you can see it.

---

Ffion Meares wrote to me in April. Third time in one lifetime — 2005, 2011, now — and the only person who has ever come back, and never once about the thing.

It was a letter, on paper, in an envelope addressed by hand, which told me before I opened it that she had thought about how it should arrive, and the stamp was a proper stamp, first class, slightly crooked, licked and pressed by a person, and I stood in the hall and had all of that before a word, which she intended, being the only journalist I have ever met who understood that the envelope is the first sentence. She is doing a long piece on the town. Not on the strike. On the town: the street, the months after, what happens to a place that the world's file closes on. She has been three times, she said, which I sat with for a while, because I have not been once, and I own a folder.

She asked one question. The inquiry's account uses a figure for those present in the adjacent buildings, and she has reason to think the figure is wrong, and she asked, with complete courtesy, whether I had ever heard a different figure used privately, adding that she understood I might not be able to answer, and that she would take no reply as a reply.

Enclosed was a list. Twenty-two names, typed, with ages, compiled from the town's own records and her three visits and the work of a Russian colleague she did not name for the colleague's sake. She sent it, she wrote, not as evidence but because she had found that officials engage differently with names than with figures, and then she apologised for the sentence, saying I was not an official and she had no business assuming the tactic would be needed.

It was needed. She knew me in 2005 for forty minutes and she has my number to a decimal place.

The fourth name on the list is Yelena Baryshnikova, a schoolteacher, who was fifty-four.

I read the list once, standing up, at the window. Then I took no reply as a reply, as offered.

I drafted nothing. I want that noticed, because the reader has watched me draft two letters to Ray Nunn and knows my habits by now. For Ffion Meares there was no draft, because a draft is a door left ajar, and she is the one correspondent I have ever had who would feel the draught. The list is in the second drawer, under this page, and it has been there since April, and I have not shown it to Martin, and I could not begin to write down the reasoning, because there is no reasoning, there is only the drawer, which has been the answer to every question of this kind since 1962.

There was a state dinner once, years after the label changed, at which a man was being witty about regulators, and I have refused all through this book to describe what happened in me while he talked. I looked at the age on the fourth line of Ffion Meares's list and I understood that the refusals are the truest map of me that anybody will ever hold, and that you are holding it.

---

The paper ran the Grantham chapter at Easter, ahead of publication, because the publisher is nervous about time, and everybody was gracious enough to pretend the nervousness was about the market.

It did what extracts are for. It was read out on the radio.

We heard it together, by accident, on the Sunday, in the kitchen — an actress doing me better than I do me — and when she reached Lorna and the chairs Martin stopped stirring and stood with his head down, listening, and at the end he said, 'You got her exactly,' which I had. Then he took his tea through. And I stood with the radio still on, having been praised for the accuracy of the half I did not invent, by the only other witness, who heard nothing wrong in the other half, because the other half was built by a professional not to be heard. Three people wrote to say it had made them cry, one of them a bishop. The passage about Lorna's chairs was quoted everywhere, accurately, which pleased me in a way I am not proud of, since I know which of its sentences are load-bearing and which are invented, and the invented ones travelled furthest.

In May a letter came through the publisher.

It was from Lorna Hollick, four lines, in the hand of a woman who has written to parents for thirty years: clear, brief, kind in structure rather than in phrasing. The extract said Ryan was recruited at twenty-two. He was twenty-three. She gave the month and the year. She asked that it be right in the book, if there was time.

That was all. Nothing about the room. Nothing about the chapter's account of the room, which she had plainly read, every invented syllable of it. Nothing about me. I read it four times looking for myself in it and I am not in it, and the reason I am not in it is the reason I have been getting wrong since the summer of the strike, and I got it at last, standing in the morning room with her letter: I am not a figure in that woman's life. Her son is. I have spent two years arranging myself around Lorna Hollick, braced for her judgement or hungry for her absolution, neither of which was ever coming, because from the fixed point where she lives I am part of the weather, and the weather does not get letters, the weather gets a date corrected, so that the record of her boy is right.

The date has been corrected. It was the last change accepted before the book went to press. The sentence I invented about her daughter stands four sentences away from it, checked by nobody, permanent.

I have put her letter in the drawer, with the list, under this. The drawer is becoming the true book. I suspected it on the first page and I know it now.

---

Two things from the house, because the house went on being a house.

Gillian drove Pat over on the Tuesday.

I heard the car and got the wrong idea, because it was not Pat's engine, and then the door, and then Pat's voice in the hall doing the talking for two, and then a woman I had not seen since her own wedding came in carrying the bag, put it down by the good chair, said, 'Hello, Alison,' as though we did this weekly, and took the window seat with her phone.

Ten years. Ten years of a war Pat could not end, because ending it meant conceding the matter, and the matter, as I have said, was one Gillian was right about, and Pat knew, and the knowing is what kept it going, the way it keeps everything in this book going.

Nobody announced anything. That wants recording, because I have sat through a great many settlements, and the good ones all look like this. Pat talked for an hour — the book, the bulbs, a scandal at the garden centre — about everything except the driver, and the driver sat in the window like a bored chauffeur and said four things, and two of them were funny, and the funnier of the two was at Pat's expense, and Pat laughed. That was the treaty. A daughter making the old joke, and a mother laughing at it, in front of a witness, so that it counted.

At the door, going, Pat said, 'She's parked round the corner, she'll bring it up,' in the voice of a woman who has been driven about all her life.

And Gillian, behind her, on the step, said to me, quietly, not for Pat: 'She talks about you the whole way home. Both ways, actually.'

I said, 'She talks about you the rest of the time.'

It was true, and I had never planned to give it to either of them, and Gillian looked at me for a moment the way her mother has never once looked at me in forty-five years — which is as though she had heard exactly what was said — and then she went and brought the car round.

Wars on that scale do not end with an instrument of surrender. They end with somebody bringing the car round. I said nothing further, which for once required nothing.

And Josie and I had our fight, the only one, in the last week of May, about my mother.

She came in on the Tuesday with the running order and did not sit down, which she always does, and I understood that we had arrived.

She said the book has a hole where 1994 should be.

I said the book has my mother where 1994 should be. Chapter one. The ochre, the sink, the hedge.

She had rehearsed the next part. I could hear the rehearsal, which moved me and armed me in equal measure. She said, 'You've given them the hand that never quite lands on the shoulder. You've given them the nurse's name and what she said at four o'clock. You have never once said what you said back, and every reader will feel the hole, because you taught them to feel it. You taught them in chapter one.'

That is a very good argument. I have spent three months helping to build the mind that makes arguments like that, and it was now in the room being used on me, which is the oldest story in this book.

I said no.

She asked why not. Pen down. Professionally.

And the true answer came up into my mouth, and the true answer is that the only thing that happened in that room is a thing that did not happen, and there are no sentences for an absence, and if I manufacture some they will be worked, because everything I make is worked, and my mother is not going to be worked, at this late stage, by me.

What I said was: 'You will do it anyway when I can't argue. So do it now, while I can.'

She went white. I watched it land — that I had accused her, with three months' notice, of the thing she will in fact have to do, and that the accusation was also permission, and that permission handed over as cruelty is a thing you cannot give back. She stood there holding the running order, and then she did her job. She did it that week.

The pages exist. They are called *The Side Room* and they are in the book and they are very moving and they are made, professionally, from things I never quite said, the way a good stock is made from bones. She wanted one passage of her own in one of these books, one passage read aloud at the festivals, and she has earned it nine times over, and she has it now, and it is the only part of *A Quiet Kind of Life* that I have declined to read, and we have never mentioned it since, and she brings the biscuits up herself these days, which is an apology, which I accept every morning by eating one, which is a conversation, conducted in biscuits, and neither of us would call it one.

---

Last thing, and then the hard chapter.

I read back through this drawer copy on Sunday, the whole of it, five hours, which I do not have to spend and spent. I was checking for something and I did not find it and I found something else.

Every page he is on, he is lit the same way. The radio he cannot work. The tea too far away. The jar. The question in Cumbria. *That was for her.* I wrote all of that in the mornings, in my clearest hours, for no audience I can name, with nothing to gain, and it is the warmest writing I have ever done, and on Sunday I sat with it and understood that I cannot verify it. I have spent a lifetime being able to hear the difference between a sentence I mean and a sentence that merely sounds like me, and on the one subject where it matters most, at the end, with the drawer open and nobody watching, I cannot tell whether I have written my husband or written the feeling of having loved my husband, and there is no test. I ran every test I know on Sunday. There is no test.

I ran the only test there is, which is substitution. I rewrote one paragraph about him coldly, as an exercise, at the table, to see whether the cold version rang truer — and it rang like a coin on wood, false as a filing, and I fed it to the shredder Josie brought in March, which has otherwise eaten nothing but drafts of the acknowledgements, and I sat listening to the machine finish and thought: there is my finding, for whatever a finding is worth from an instrument testing itself.

I am not revising a word of it. I have decided to believe it.

Note the verb. I taught you the verb in the second chapter, and I have used it on my own age, and on my husband's silence, and on a coincidence of counties, and on the finder of this drawer, and now on forty-two years of marriage, and if you want the finding of this book in one line, it is that a woman can get to the end having decided nearly everything and been overtaken by nothing, and that the file on whether that is a life still comes back the way my file always came back.

Not inconsistent with one.

### Seventeen

I have the diary for 1996.

It is the ordinary kind, week to an opening, a bridge on the cover, the same series as the one that lived in the drawer on my side of the bed through the trying years, because I bought them from the same shop for thirty years until the shop went, and a woman keeps her stationery loyalties longer than most vows, and I went up and got it myself on Monday, which I am not supposed to do, stairs being what they now are, and I sat with the third week of September open on my knee for most of an afternoon.

That was the week. I can say that now with the whole life visible the way you can stand on the cliff and see the fen: if there was ever a week in forty-two years when leaving would have been easy, it was that one. The fourth cycle had failed in the February. The trying was over in everything but the saying. He was in Nottingham four days of the seven, absorbed, kind on the telephone, gone. Christine would have taken me in that same night and asked nothing for a month. I was forty-three, I had money of my own, I was four years from the top of my profession, and there was nothing in front of me that week but the week.

Here is what the diary says. Monday: *tables to R by 12* — a piece of work I remember, and remember being pleased with, the lag analysis that became the method everyone used after. It is still in use. I looked it up last winter, in the way you look up an old address, and it is a standard method now with two other people's names on it, correctly, because they generalised it, and I minded for about an hour, which at my age is a sprint, and then I did not mind, because a method with no name on it that everybody uses is the closest thing my profession has to a hedge that outlives the household, and I had, after all, been trained from birth for exactly that. Wednesday: *Pat, 1, Fortes* — I remember the lunch; Pat was at war with a neighbour then, the rehearsal war, before Gillian. Friday: *17.04 Newark* — I went up to see Colin about the headstone wording, and came back Sunday, and the wording we chose was fine.

There is one more entry that week, in the Saturday square, in pencil, one word: *roof*. It is not a metaphor. Colin and I went up into our mother's loft that Saturday, because the house was being sold, and handed things down to each other for two hours, and at one point he said, from above, 'Catch,' and dropped the biscuit tin with Whitby on it — hers, not Maureen's; everyone of that generation had one — and I caught it, and we laughed, both of us, loudly, in the dust, and it was the only time in the whole business of her death that either of us laughed, and I had forgotten it entirely until the diary handed it back, which is what diaries are for, and what this book may also be for, on its better mornings. That is not the sentence I could not give Josie. But it is allowed to sit beside it.

And that is all it says, and I have sat with it, and here is the finding. I was not deciding to stay that week. I have gone into the week the way I have gone into every document in this book, looking for the deliberation, the weighing, the moment on the platform at Newark, and there is nothing to find, because no deliberation occurred. The place in me where the question *should I go* would have had to stand is not a room with a closed door. It is not a room. I was never in the corridor. I did the tables, and had the lunch, and caught the 17.04, and the largest question of my life was never asked anywhere inside me, by anyone, and you cannot call a thing a choice that was never present anywhere as a question.

Marianne thought I stayed out of fear. Hannah thinks I stayed out of sacrifice. They have both walked round a decision that does not exist, like tourists round a monument that was never built, and I have let them, because their versions require me to have been in the corridor, and being in the corridor is the dignified thing, and the truth is a woman on a train doing the crossword.

---

Neil. I said he would come into this book once, and this is the once, and he gets four sentences.

Neil asked us for money in 2011, a great deal of it, for the second time, and we said no, and the voice that said it was mine, in our kitchen, with Martin sitting there letting me, which we have never discussed. The first time had been about Doreen's house, in 2006, and what was done then is covered by nine words that Martin and I agreed never to say, and I am not going to say them here either, and you may sit with the fact that there is one sentence this book declines that even the drawer does not get. He took it the way he takes things, which is entirely, and at Christmas he sends a card, and we send one, and that is the whole traffic of thirty years. I have used him twice in this book as a thing I could have thrown on a Tuesday morning, and that is a shabby use of a brother-in-law, and it is also exactly what he was to me by then, which is the more shabby fact, and both of them stay in.

Now.

I have been sitting here for some time with the machine off, because the machine is Josie's and this is not, and I notice that I do not know which of you I am talking to any more. There have been two of you all along. There is the one who bought the book, who I have teased and served and led by the hand for sixteen chapters, and there is the one who opened a drawer in a morning room, who was owed something better than teasing, and I have been switching between you without marking it, chapter by chapter, sometimes inside a sentence, and I cannot any longer feel the switch. Perhaps that is the hand going, or the hours. Perhaps there was only ever one of you, which would mean the book reached the drawer, which is a thought I am not going to have at this time of the morning.

Whichever one of you is there. This next part is yours.

---

It was twenty to seven, and the cold cup was on the table, and I have told you what was available. Neil, and the money, and the nine words. Any of them would have gone in deeper. None of them would have gone in true.

Because here is what those three have in common, and it has taken me the whole book to be able to set it side by side: they are all his failures. Every one of them is a place where he fell short and knows it. And a man being struck on his failures closes over them, like the conversation over the aircraft, and endures it, and forgives you by Thursday, because somewhere underneath he has already convicted himself and your blow lands on a done case.

The dishwasher was not a failure. The dishwasher was the thing he had done.

He had been up since four, with the folder, with the worst page of his life in the study, and at some point in that darkness he had come down and loaded the machine and run it, badly, the knives up, the cups wrong, because it was ten past five and his wife was asleep and it was the one part of the morning he could put right with his hands. It was not a chore. I have never once let myself write the true noun for what it was. It was an offering. Small, and botched, and set out for me in a basket, points in the air, the way a cat leaves you something it caught.

And I came down, slept and clear and level, and out of the whole morning I chose that.

Not the failures. The offering. And I did it in the register.

You know the register. My mother built it at a sink on Cliff Rise and handed it to me with the knives, and I have carried it through every room in this book and never once taken it down, mild, and warm, and impossible to answer, the register that does not accuse a man but reclassifies him, three cups at a time, from a husband into a boy being shown how —

and the sentence is gone, I have told you the sentence is gone, but the register is not gone, the register is right here, I could do it to you now, whoever you are, I could do it in nine words about the way you are holding these pages —

That is what his face was. I have been asked four times what his face was if it was not anger, and I have never said, and it was this: it was the face of a man watching his offering be received as a mess. Reclassified in front of him, points and all. He looked at the open machine, and at me, and something was put away that never came out again, and he said, 'All right,' and the folder was in the study, and the car came at eight minutes past.

I did not plan it. Nothing in this book was ever planned; that has been the whole horror of the book. But hands do not need plans. Hands have inventories. And on the fourteenth of May, at twenty to seven, my hand went along the whole shelf of that marriage, past everything blunt, and came back with the one thing in the house that was sharp because it was tender, and I used it on a tired man I loved, about cutlery, and then I finished emptying the machine, and the knives went somewhere, and I have no memory of putting them away.

The garden wants doing. Josie comes at half nine. The surveyor, I am told, is now expected on Thursday, though Trisha has been careful to commit to nothing, and Martin is drafting, aloud, in the next room, the remarks he intends to make to the surveyor about the length of the wait, and they are very funny, and he will not make them, and the tea will be here at half past ten, slightly too far away.

I am going to sit in this window until then.

### Eighteen

It is early as I write this. The house has the five-o'clock silence I have written half this book inside, and the hand is slow, and I am underlining more of the words than I am sure of, which is a change of policy I have not discussed with myself.

He came in on the Thursday with the post and the good scissors, because a parcel was expected, and there was no parcel, and he stayed.

Josie was reading me the running order for the last part of the book, which is shorter than the running order for a life ought to be, and he sat on the arm of the sofa the way he is not supposed to, and listened, and at the chapter called *A Tuesday* he made a small sound, and Josie, because I have taught her nothing, asked him whether he remembered the morning itself.

He said not really, you know. It was all the folder by then. He said the drive he remembered, and the statement he remembered, and the middle of the day was forty phone calls in a row.

And then he laughed, before the laugh had anything attached to it, the way he does now, and said, 'I'll tell you what I do remember. I come out of all that — that whole morning, worst morning of the job, bar none — I get back that night, and I get a telling-off about the knives.' He turned to Josie, delighted, building it. 'The dishwasher. I'd loaded it wrong, apparently. Points up. After the morning I'd had! I'm standing there thinking, I have had the morning of all mornings, and I am being shown how to stack cutlery.' He shook his head, fond of it, fond of us. 'Forty years. She never let a thing slide in forty years, and thank God, because I'd have been a disgrace.'

Josie laughed. It is a genuinely funny telling. He has the timing of a man who spent decades at other people's dinners, and the detail of the points-up is doing exactly the work he wants it to do, which is to make the great morning small and the small marriage great.

She asked whether she could use it. He said he didn't see why not — 'shows nobody's grand at home, doesn't it' — and looked at me, checking, the way he checks, and I smiled, and the smile was counted as consent, and it is consent, and it will be in the book, at the end of the Tuesday chapter, filling the space where the kitchen was.

His version has the argument in the evening. After. A telling-off when he got home, a wife keeping a great man's feet on the ground, a marriage in good order. It is the version a mind makes when it files a thing under comedy, and he filed it that night, I think, or in the weeks after, when nothing came and the country settled, and he has carried it that way for two years, and it costs him nothing, and it is wrong by twelve hours, and the twelve hours are the book you are holding.

I am the only person alive who knows the order. I am the record. And I sat in my chair with the sun coming across the carpet and I heard the record being amended in front of me, cheerfully, by its principal witness, for an audience of one professional, and I said nothing, and I want the reason on this page because it is the last reason I will get to give: there is no version of correcting him that is not, itself, the register. *Actually, darling, it was the morning.* Mild. Warm. Impossible to answer. I would be reclassifying his best anecdote in front of a young woman with a recorder, three cups at a time, and I have done that to him once, and once is what this book is about.

Josie stayed on after he had gone, longer than the session, sorting pages that did not need sorting, and at the door she said, not looking at me, 'It's a good story. The knives.' And I said it was. And she said, 'Mm,' in a way I have heard before, from Nadine, in a corridor — and she has the Friday files, all of them, the four days of the kitchen, the fourteenth of May in my own voice, and she is the most truthful person I have ever worked with, and she had just told me, in one syllable, deniably, the way every true thing has been said to me my whole life, that she knows his version has it the wrong way round, and that it is going in anyway, because it is charming, and because charm is what fills holes. Then she left, and I sat for a while with the last item of the evening, and put it where I put things.

So the wrong version goes in the book, in his voice, charming, and the right version goes in the drawer, in mine, and the two of everything hold, all the way to the end.

Housekeeping, while the hand allows.

The boxes in the roof stay in the roof — the letters, the hardback notebook with the forty-one dates, the print of Market Towers. Unopened is its own kind of filing, and it is mine.

The drawer, then, since I promised you the order. From the bottom: Denise's card. The folder on Ryan Hollick. Ffion's list. Lorna's letter. The green casework notebook, which goes in tonight — nine hundred outcomes as Dorrington, the shortest document in the drawer and the only one with no sentences in it. And these pages on top, which is where you found them.

The last Friday file went yesterday. I heard the printer do its work in the boot room, further away than it used to sound, and Josie brought the pages through and squared them on the table, and said, 'That's the set,' and neither of us said anything ceremonial, because we have between us perhaps the only working relationship in England with no ceremony in it anywhere, and I signed the release her contract wants, and she put it in the folder with the rubber band, and the rubber band, I noticed, was the same one, four months old, kept, because she is Josie, and some people you simply hand the world to and it comes back squared.

The book is done, or done enough. Josie has what she needs for the last chapters if I stop being able to give it to her, which was agreed in March, at the same meeting where the schedule was agreed, and she cried at that meeting, once, briefly, and then wrote down what I said, which is the whole of Josie, and I would not have got through this year with anybody else, and I have told her so, this morning, out loud, to her visible alarm.

She recovered by asking whether that was for the book. I said it was for the room. She wrote it down anyway, in pencil.

Josie tidies this room on Fridays and has never once opened the second drawer, because I asked her not to, in March. For the handkerchiefs, I said. She believed me. She is the most truthful person I have ever worked with, and when the time comes she will do exactly what she has been asked, which is to clear the room, and leave the table, because the table is spoken for. The table goes to Hannah. It is in the will, itemised, the little table from the morning room, and nobody will think anything of it, an aunt leaving a niece a table.

Hannah opens drawers. She has done it since she was nine, at Colin's, at Christmas, not from nosiness but from a kind of trust in the world, a certainty that whatever is in there will be interesting and that nobody would really mind. Nobody minded. Nobody has ever minded anything Hannah did, which is its own subject, and it is too late for me to take up new subjects.

She asked me in March whether I ever minded, and I told her the kind lie, and she believed me, and one of these years, in her thirties still I should think, she will get the table home and go through it the way she goes through everything, expecting spare buttons.

I have thought about what that afternoon will be for her, and I have decided —

I have decided it is hers.

The bulbs Hannah planted in March are up. Wrong colours, she warns me, possibly; the packets got mixed. I have told her I have no preference, which, for once, is true.

The tea came at half past ten. He put it down slightly too far away, and I looked at it, and I looked at him going out with the post under his arm, talking about Thursday and the surveyor — and the surveyor came, on the Thursday, I will close that account while I can: twenty-six, terrified, thorough, and Martin made him tea and delivered not one word of the drafted remarks, and pronounced afterwards that the lad knew his stuff, and the fence, for it was at bottom about the fence, will outlast us all, which is the point of fences, and of surveys, and, it turns out, of Thursdays — and I reached the extra distance, the way I have reached it for thirty-one years, and the reach is the marriage, and I could have said so at any point since 1995, and there is no time now to start saying things, and the tea was hot, and he had put it in the blue cup.

Anything sharp facing down.
